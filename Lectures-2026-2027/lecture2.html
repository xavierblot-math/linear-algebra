Lecture 2 : sum, direct sum and complements

In the last lecture we introduced the notion of vector space and vector subspace, together with basic facts. In this lecture we study fundamental operations we can perform with vector spaces.

Intersection of vector subspaces

Intersection of vector subspaces

Let \(F,G\subset E\) be vector subspaces of a vector space \( E\). Then \(F\cap G\) is also a vector subspace of \( E\). Indeed:

  • \(0\in F\cap G\).
  • If \(x,y\in F\cap G\), then \(x+y\in F\) and \(x+y\in G\), since both \( F\) and \( G\) are vector subspaces.
  • For any \(\lambda\in\mathbb{K}\) and \( x\in F\cap F\), \(\lambda x\in F\) and \(\lambda x\in G\).

More generally, for an arbitrary family \(\{F_i\}_{i\in I}\) of subspaces of \( E\), by the same token, the intersection \(\displaystyle \bigcap_{i\in I} F_i\) is a subspace of \( E\).

Example: coordinate planes in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)

In \(E=\mathbb{R}^3\), the \(xy\)-plane is the subspace \(F=\mathbb{R}^2\times\{0\}\), and the \(yz\)-plane is the subspace \(G=\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\). They intersect in the $y$-axis, namely \(F\cap G=\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}\times\{0\}\).

Sum of vector spaces

Definition

For subspaces \(F,G\subset E\), we define \[ F+G \;:=\; \{\,u+v\in E \mid u\in F,\ v\in G\,\}, \] and we call it the sum of \( F\) and \(G\).

Lemma
Let the notation be as above.
  1. \(F+G\) is a subspace of \(E\).
  2. \(F\subset F+G\) and \(G\subset F+G\).
  3. Universal property: if \(H\subset E\) is a subspace with \(F\subset H\) and \(G\subset H\), then \(F+G\subset H\).

Proof

Below we use letters \(u\) for vectors in \(F\) and letters \(v\) for vectors in \(G\).

(a) We have \(0=0+0\in F+G\) and \((u_1+v_1)+(u_2+v_2)=(u_1+u_2)+(v_1+v_2)\in F+G\). Similarly, for every \(\lambda\in\mathbb{K}\) we have \(\lambda(u+v)=(\lambda u)+(\lambda v)\in F+G\).

(b) Clear since \(u=u+0\) and \(v=0+v\).

(c) If \(F,G\subset H\) then \(u+v\in H\), hence \(F+G\subset H\).

Generalization: sum of a family of vector subspaces

For subspaces \(\{F_i\}_{i\in I}\) of $E$, we define \[ \sum_{i\in I}F_i \;:=\; \left\{\,u_{i_1}+\cdots+u_{i_\ell}\ \middle|\ u_{i_j}\in F_{i_j}\ \text{for some finite } \{i_1,\dots,i_\ell\}\subset I \right\}, \]

and we call it the sum of the subspaces \(\{F_i\}_{i\in I}\). It satisfies analogous properties to the case of two subspaces.

Example: sum of coordinate planes and axes in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)

In \(\mathbb{R}^3\), the sum of any two distinct coordinate planes is \(\mathbb{R}^3\). For instance, we can write \[ (x,y,z)=(x,y,0)+(0,0,z), \] showing that \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\) is the sum of the \(xy\)-plane and the \(yz\)-plance. By the same token, \(\mathbb{R}^3\) is the sum of the \(xy\)-plane and the \(z\)-axis.

Example: two more interesting planes whose sum is \(\mathbb{R}^3\)

Let \[ F=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^3\mid x+2y=0\},\qquad G=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^3\mid x-y+z=0\}. \] Then \(F+G=\mathbb{R}^3\). Indeed, one has \((-2,1,0), (0,0,1)\in F\) and \((1,1,0),(-1,0,1)\in G\). From \[(1,1,0)-(-2,1,0)=(3,0,0)\] we get \[(1,0,0)=\tfrac13\big((1,1,0)-(-2,1,0)\big)\in F+G\] Similarly \((0,1,0)=\tfrac13\big(2(1,1,0)+(-2,1,0)\big)\in F+G\). We already know that \( (0,0,1)\in F\subset F+G\). Since any vector in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) is a combination of \( (1,0,0), (0,1,0), (0,0,1)\), we infer that \(F+G=\mathbb{R}^3\).

Direct sums

Above we saw that $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ is the sum of the $xy$-plane and the $yz$-plane, but also the sum of the $xy$-plane and the $z$-axis. There is a substantial difference between these two situations. The $xy$-plane and the $yz$-plane intersect along the $y$-axis, i.e. they share a direction. By contrast, the intersection of the $xy$-plane and the $z$-axis is reduces at 0. These spaces do not share any direction. In such situation, we speak of direct sum.
Lemma
Let the notation be as above. The following conditions are equivalent:
  1. \(F\cap G=\{0\}\).
  2. For all $u\in F+G$, there exist unique $f\in F$ and $g\in G$ such that $u=f+g$.

Proof

(a) Suppose first that the intersection is $0$. Let $u=f+g$, with $f\in F$ and $g\in G$. Suppose we have another such decomposition $u=f^{\prime}+g^{\prime}$. Then \[ f-f^{\prime}=g^{\prime}-g. \] Now, the left hand side belongs to $F$, and the right hand side belongs to $G$. Hence, both sides belong to $F\cap G$. Since the latter is zero, we infer $f=f^{\prime}$ and $g=g^{\prime}$.

(b) If $u\in F\cap G$, then we decompose $u=u+0=0+u$. Using the uniqueness of the decomposition, we conclude that $u=0$.

Definition

Let $F, G$ be subspaces of a vector space $E$. If any of the equivalent conditions of the previous lemma holds, then we say that $F$ and $G$ form a direct sum. In this case, we write $F\oplus G$ instead of $F+G$. The symbol $\oplus$ hence means $+$ together with the condition $F\cap G=\lbrace 0\rbrace$.

Examples
  1. Recall: a polynomial of degree \(d \geq 0\) in \(\mathbb{K}[X]\) has at most \(d\) distinct roots.

    In \(\mathbb{K}[X]_{\leq 3}\), let:

    • \(F = \{ P \in \mathbb{K}[X]_{\leq 3} \mid P(0) = P(1) = 0 \}\)
    • \(G = \{ P \in \mathbb{K}[X]_{\leq 3} \mid P(2) = P(3) = 0 \}\)

    Then, by the property above, \(F \cap G = \{0\}\).

    As a tedious exercise, you can try to show that \[ F \oplus G = \mathbb{K}[X]_{\leq 3}. \] We will later provide useful tools (dimension theory) which provide an simple approach.

  2. In \(C^0(\mathbb{R}) = \{ f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R} \ \text{continuous}\}\), let:

    • \(F_0 = \{ f \in C^0(\mathbb{R}) \mid f(0)=0 \}\)
    • \(F_1 = \{ f \in C^0(\mathbb{R}) \mid f(1)=0 \}\)

    Then \(F_0 \cap F_1 \neq \{0\}\), since \(f(x) = \sin(\pi x)\) belongs to both \(F_0\) and \(F_1\), and \(f \neq 0\).

    As an exercise, try to show that \[ F_0 + F_1 = C^0(\mathbb{R}). \]

Complements of vector subspaces

Definition

Let \(E\) be a vector space and \(F \subset E\) a vector subspace. A complement for \(F\) is a vector subspace \(G \subset E\) such that

\[ E = F \oplus G. \]

We then say that \(F\) and \(G\) are complementary vector subspaces.

Proposition

Let \(E\) be a vector space. Then any vector subspace \(F \subset E\) admits a complement.

Proof

In general, a proof based on Zorn's Lemma is proposed in Exercise 13 of Exercise Sheet 2.

We will give another proof later when we discuss dimensions and bases, for finite-dimensional vector spaces.

Span of a family of vectors

Definition

Let \(A=\{e_i\}_{i \in I}\) be a family of vectors in a vector space \(E\). We define the span of $A$, denoted by

\[ \mathrm{Vect}(A)\quad\text{or}\quad\langle A \rangle \]

to be the set of all finite linear combinations \[ \lambda_1 e_{i_1} + \cdots + \lambda_n e_{i_n}, \quad \lambda_j \in \mathbb{K},\ i_j \in I, \] where $n$ is not fixed.

For the empty family \(\varnothing\), we set \(\mathrm{Vect}(\varnothing) = \langle \varnothing \rangle = \{0\}.\)

Lemma

Let $A=\lbrace e_{i}\rbrace_{i\in I}$ be a family of vectors in a vector space $E$. Then:

  1. \(\langle A\rangle\) is a vector subspace, and $A\subset\(\langle A\rangle\).
  2. If \(H \subset E\) is a vector subspace and \(A\subset H\), then $\langle A\rangle\subset H$.
Proof

Left as an exercise.

Examples
  1. \(\mathbb{R}^3 = \langle (1,0,0), (0,1,0), (0,0,1) \rangle\).
  2. If $F$ and $G$ are vector subspaces of $E$, then \(F + G = \langle F \cup G \rangle\).
  3. \(\mathbb{K}[X]_{\leq d} = \langle 1, X, X^2, \ldots, X^d \rangle\).
  4. \(\mathbb{K}[X] = \langle 1, X, X^2, \ldots \rangle\).
