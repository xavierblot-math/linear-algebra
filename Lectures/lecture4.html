Lecture 4 : bases and dimension

In the previous lectures we have been dealing with linear independence, the span of a family of vector spaces, and the notion of dimension. These notions are actually related. For example, we saw that in a vector space of dimension $n$, any spanning family of cardinality $n$ is linear independent. We wish to study such special families of vectors, and along the way provide tools to determine the dimension of a vector space.

Examples and definition

First examples of bases

In many examples such as $\mathbb K^n$ and $\mathbb K[X]$, we have exhibited families of vectors with the aforementioned fundamental properties:

  1. Spanning family.
    $$\mathbb K^n=\langle (1,0,\ldots,0),\ldots,(0,\ldots,0,1)\rangle,\qquad \mathbb K[X]=\langle 1,X,X^2,\ldots\rangle.$$
    Hence any vector can be written as a combination of such vectors.
  2. Linear independence. The displayed spanning families are also linear independent. Consequently, any vector can be uniquely written in terms of such vectors, up to order.

These are examples of bases, and they can be seen as the building blocks of a vector space, which encapsulate all the algebraic structure of it.

Definition (Basis)

Let $E$ be a $\mathbb K$-vector space. A basis of $E$ is a family of vectors $\lbrace e_i\rbrace_{i\in I}$ such that:

  1. It is a spanning family for $E$, that is $E=\langle\{e_i\}_{i\in I}\rangle$;
  2. It is linearly independent.

Concretely, for every $v\in E$ there exist $e_{i_1},\ldots,e_{i_\ell}$ from the family and unique coefficients $\lambda_{i_1},\ldots,\lambda_{i_\ell}\in\mathbb K$ such that $$v=\lambda_{i_1}e_{i_1}+\cdots+\lambda_{i_\ell}e_{i_\ell}.$$ Furthermore, if $v\neq 0$ and we require the $\lambda_{i_j}$ to be non-zero, then the subfamily $e_{i_1},\ldots,e_{i_\ell}$ is unique, up to order.

Existence, cardinality and dimension

Theorem (Existence of bases)

Every vector space admits a basis.

Proof

If $E$ is finite dimensional with $\dim E=n$ and $E=\langle e_1,\ldots,e_n\rangle$, we know from the previous lecture that $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ are linearly independent. For general $E$, not necessarily finite dimensional, we admit the result. See Exercise Sheet 3.

Theorem (Dimension and cardinality of bases)

Let $E$ be a finite dimensional $\mathbb K$-vector space. Suppose $\dim E=n$. Then any basis has exactly $n$ elements.

The proof of the theorem above will occupy the rest of this lecture. We divide it in several intermediate statements, of independent interest.

Lemma (A)

Let $u_1,\ldots,u_m\in E$ be linearly independent vectors in a vector space. Then, for any $k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}$,

$$\langle u_1,\ldots,u_k\rangle\ \cap\ \langle u_{k+1},\ldots,u_m\rangle=\{0\}.$$
Proof

If $u$ lies in the intersection, write $u=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_i u_i=\sum_{i=k+1}^{m}\lambda_i u_i$. Rearranging gives

$$\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}u_{i}+\sum_{i=k+1}^{m}(-\lambda_{i})u_{i}=0. $$

By linear independence, we infer $\lambda_{i}=0$ for all $i$, hence $u=0$.

Lemma (B)

Let $u_1,\ldots,u_m\in E$ be vectors in a vector space, and fix $k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}$. Let $v_{k+1},\ldots,v_m\in\langle u_1,\ldots,u_k\rangle$. Then

$$\langle u_1,\ldots,u_m\rangle =\langle u_1,\ldots,u_k,\ u_{k+1}+v_{k+1},\ldots, u_m+v_m\rangle.$$
Proof

Inclusion $\supseteq$ is clear. For $\subseteq$ note $u_{k+j}=(u_{k+j}+v_{k+j})-v_{k+j}$ with $v_{k+j}\in\langle u_1,\ldots,u_k\rangle$.

Lemma (C)

Let $u_1,\ldots,u_m\in E$ be linearly independent vectors in a vector space. Fix $k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}$ and take $v_{k+1},\ldots,v_m\in\langle u_1,\ldots,u_k\rangle$. Then the family $$u_1,\ldots,u_k,\ u_{k+1}+v_{k+1},\ldots,u_m+v_m$$ is linearly independent.

Consider a linear combination

$$\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_i u_i+\sum_{i=k+1}^{m}\lambda_i(u_i+v_i)=0.$$

Write the $v_{i}$ in terms of $u_{1},\ldots, u_{k}$, and then separate the above combination into the parts in $\langle u_1,\ldots,u_k\rangle$ and in $\langle u_{k+1},\ldots,u_m\rangle$. By the Lemma (A), the intersection of these spaces is $\{0\}$; linear independence forces first $\lambda_{k+1}=\cdots=\lambda_m=0$, and then $\lambda_1=\cdots=\lambda_k=0$.

The lemmas above allow us to prove the following proposition, which actually implies the theorem on dimension and cardinality of bases.

Proposition (D)

Let $E$ be a $\mathbb K$-vector space, and let $u_1,\ldots,u_m\in E$ and $e_1,\ldots,e_n\in E$ be linearly independent families of vectors. Assume that $\langle u_1,\ldots,u_m\rangle\subseteq\langle e_1,\ldots,e_n\rangle$. Then $m\le n,$ and the equality $m=n$ holds if, and only if, we have an equality $\langle u_1,\ldots,u_m\rangle=\langle e_1,\ldots,e_n\rangle$.

Proof

We begin with a sketch of the proof, to be detailed below. The idea is to write each $u_i$ as a linear combination of the $e_j$, and then perform a Gaussian-elimination–style process using the lemmas above to replace some $u_i$ by $u_{i}'=u_i+v_i$ for some $v_{i}$, while preserving span and independence. This is done in a way that $u_{i}'$ is of the form $\alpha_{i}e_{i}+\alpha_{i+1}e_{i+1}+\ldots $, with $\alpha_{i}\neq 0$. Linear independence then implies that $m\leq n$. If $m=n$, one can reverse the procedure, in order to write the $e_{i}$ in terms of the $u_{j}$.

A proof by Guassian elimination

We next elaborate on the proof of Proposition (D) , which serves as an excuse to introduce the Guassian elimination method.

Gaussian elimination method

It is enough to prove that if $m\geq n$, then necessarily $m=n$ and $\langle u_{1},\ldots, u_{n}\rangle=\langle e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}\rangle$.

We focus on the first $n$ vectors $u_{1},\ldots, u_{n}$. We write

\[ \begin{aligned} u_1 &= a_1 e_1 + a_2 e_2 + \cdots + a_n e_n,\\ u_2 &= b_1 e_1 + b_2 e_2 + \cdots + b_n e_n,\\ u_3 &= c_1 e_1 + c_2 e_2 + \cdots + c_n e_n,\\ & \hspace{0.25cm}\vdots \\ u_n &= d_1 e_1 + d_2 e_2 + \cdots + d_n e_n.\\ \end{aligned} \]

Note that one would more usually write the coefficients in the form $a_{ij}$, where $i$ refers to the vector $e_{i}$ and $j$ to the vector $u_{j}$, or the other way round depending on the conventions. Here we avoid the double indices to lighten notation.

Step 1

Since $u_1,\ldots, u_{n}$ are linearly independent, $u_1 \ne 0$. Hence some coefficient $a_i \ne 0$. Possibly reordering the $e_i$, we may assume $a_1\ne 0$. For notational convenience, we set $u_1' = u_1$ and $a_{i}'=a_{i}$. Hence, $a_{1}'\neq 0$.

Step 2

Define $u_2' = u_2 - \dfrac{b_1}{a_1'}u_1'.$

By Lemma (B) the span is preserved

$$\langle u_1',u_2',\ldots,u_m\rangle=\langle u_1,u_2,\ldots,u_m\rangle,$$

and by Lemma (C) $u_1',u_2',\ldots,u_m$ are still linearly independent. In particular, $u_2'\neq 0$. Thus, proving the proposition for $u_1,\ldots,u_m$ is equivalent to proving it for $u_1',u_2',\ldots,u_m.$

Step 3

We inspect the newly constructed vector $u_{2}'$. We have:

\[ u_2' = u_2 - \frac{b_1}{a_1'}u_1' = 0\cdot e_1 + b_2' e_2 + \cdots + b_n' e_n, \qquad b_i' = b_i - \frac{b_1}{a_{1}'}{a_{i}'}. \]

Since $u_2'\neq 0$, possibly reordering $e_2,\ldots,e_n$, we may assume $b_2'\ne0.$ This eventual reordering does not affect $e_1$, and hence does not affect the condition $a_1\ne 0$ in Step 1.

Step 4

Now our vectors look like

\[ \begin{aligned} u_1' &= a_1' e_1 + a_2' e_2 + \cdots + a_n' e_n,\\ u_2' &= \phantom{a_1 e_1 + \ }b_2' e_2 + \cdots + b_n' e_n,\\ u_3 &= c_1 e_1 + c_2 e_2 + \cdots + c_n e_n,\\ & \hspace{0.25cm}\vdots \\ u_n &= d_1 e_1 + d_2 e_2 + \cdots + d_n e_n.\\ \end{aligned} \]

We construct a new vector $u_{3}'$ in two steps:

  • We first let $u_3^{(1)} = u_3 - \dfrac{c_1}{a_1'}u_1'$. It is of the form $c_{2}^{(1)}e_{2}+\cdots+c_{n}^{(1)}e_{n}$, for some coefficients $c_{i}^{(1)}$.
  • We then set $u_{3}'=u_{3}^{(1)}-\dfrac{c_{2}^{(1)}}{b_{2}'}u_{2}'$.

By Lemma (B) $\langle u_1',u_2',u_3,\ldots,u_m\rangle=\langle u_1',u_2',u_3',\ldots,u_m\rangle,$ and by Lemma (C) the vectors $u_1',u_2',u_3',\ldots,u_m$ remain linearly independent. In particular, $u_3'\neq 0$. Thus, proving the proposition for $u_1', u_2', u_3,\ldots,u_m$ is equivalent to proving it for $u_1',u_2',u_{3}',\ldots,u_m.$

Step 5

We inspect the newly constructed vector. It is of the form

\[ u_3' = c_3' e_3 + \cdots + c_n' e_n, \]

for some coefficients $c_{i}'$ we don't need to specify. Since $u_3'\neq 0$, we can reorder $e_3,\ldots,e_n$ and assume $c_3'\ne 0$. This reordering does not affect $e_1, e_2$, and hence does not change the fact that $a_{1}', b_{2}'\neq 0$ in Step 1 and Step 3.

Step 6

Continuing in this fashion, we obtain new vectors $u_1',u_2',\ldots,u_n'$, such that $u_{1}',u_{2},\ldots, u_{n}', u_{n+1},\ldots, u_{m}$ are linearly independent and satisfy $\langle u_1',u_{2}',\ldots,u_m\rangle\subseteq \langle e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}\rangle$. Moreover, the general shape of the first $n$ vectors $u_{i}'$ in terms of the $e_{j}$ is triangular:

\[ \begin{aligned} u_1' &= a_1' e_1 + a_2' e_2 + a_3'e_3+\cdots + a_n' e_n,\\ u_2' &= \phantom{a_1' e_1 + \ }b_2' e_2 +b_3'e_3+ \cdots + b_n' e_n,\\ u_3' &= \phantom{a_1' e_1 + b_2' e_2 + \ }c_3' e_3 + \cdots + c_n' e_n,\\ & \hspace{0.25cm}\vdots \\ u_n' &= \phantom{ a_1' e_1 + a_2' e_2 + a_3' e_3 + \cdots + \ } d_n' e_n , \end{aligned} \]

with all leading coefficients $a_1', b_2', c_2',\ldots, d_n'$ nonzero.

Step 7

Next we see that $m=n$. Assume we had a further vector $u_{n+1}$. As in Step 2 and Step 4 we construct a new vector $u_{n+1}'$, which will actually vanish. For this, write $u_{n+1}=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}e_{i}$. We proceed in $n$ steps:

  • We first define $u_{n+1}^{(1)}=u_{n+1}-\dfrac{\alpha_{1}}{a_{1}'}u_{1}'$. It is of the form $\alpha_{2}^{(1)}e_{2}+\ldots+\alpha_{n}^{(1)}e_{n}$, for some coefficients $\alpha_{i}^{(1)}$.
  • Then we define $u_{n+1}^{(2)}=u_{n+1}^{(1)}-\dfrac{\alpha_{2}^{(1)}}{b_{2}'}u_{2}'$. It is of the form $\alpha_{3}^{(2)}e_{3}+\ldots+\alpha_{n}^{(2)}e_{n}$, for some coefficients $\alpha_{i}^{(2)}$.
  • Next we let $u_{n+1}^{(3)}=u_{n+1}^{(2)}-\dfrac{\alpha_{3}^{(2)}}{c_{3}'}u_{3}'$. It is of the form $\alpha_{4}^{(3)}e_{4}+\ldots+\alpha_{n}^{(3)}e_{n}$, for some coefficients $\alpha_{i}^{(3)}$.
  • At some point we reach $u_{n+1}^{(n-1)}$ of the form $\alpha_{n}^{(n-1)}e_{n}$.
  • In the last step, we set $u_{n+1}'=u_{n+1}^{(n-1)}-\dfrac{\alpha_{n}^{(n-1)}}{d_n'}u_{n}'=0$.

But Lemma (B) asserts that the new vectors $u_{1}',\ldots, u_{n+1}'$ should be linear independent, while $u_{n+1}'=0$. This is absurd, and hence, $m=n$.

Step 8

Finally, we need to see that $\langle u_{1}',\ldots, u_{n}'\rangle=\langle e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}\rangle$. We reverse the previous reasoning, starting with $u_{n}'$ and descending to $u_{1}'$. Precisely, we write

\[ \begin{aligned} u_{n}' &=d_{n}'e_{n},\\ u_{n-1}'&=f_{n}'e_{n}+f_{n-1}' e_{n-1},\\ u_{n-2}'&=g_{n}'e_{n}+g_{n-1}'e_{n-1}+g_{n-2}e_{n-2},\\ & \hspace{0.25cm}\vdots \\ u_{1}'&=a_{n}'e_{n}+a_{n-1}'e_{n-1}+a_{n-2}'e_{n-2}+\cdots+a_{1}'.\\ \end{aligned} \]

Written in this form, the right-most coefficients $d_{n}', f_{n-1}', g_{n-2}',\ldots, a_{1}'$ are nonzero. Proceeding exactly as in Step 1 to Step 6, we can construct a new family of vectors $u_{n}'', u_{n-1}'',\ldots, u_{1}''$ with the same span as $u_{n}',\ldots, u_{1}'$, and such that $u_{k}''=\beta_{k} e_{k}$ for some $\beta_{k}\neq 0$. This means that $e_{k}=\frac{1}{\beta_{k}}u_{k}''$, and hence

$$\langle e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}\rangle=\langle u_{1}'',\ldots, u_{n}''\rangle=\langle u_{1}',\ldots, u_{n}'\rangle.$$

This concludes the proof.

The following corollary and its proof are important.
Corollary

Let $E$ be a vector space and $e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}\in E$ linearly independent vectors. Then $e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}$ is a basis of $\langle e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}\rangle$ and $\dim \langle e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}\rangle=n$

Proof

The first claim is obvious by definition of basis. We need to prove the dimension statement. We have a chain of inclusions

\[ \langle e_{1}\rangle \subset \langle e_{1},e_{2}\rangle\subset\cdots \subset\langle e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}\rangle. \]

By the linear independence, the inclusions are strict, and by Proposition (D) the respective dimensions satisfy

\[ 1=\dim \langle e_{1}\rangle < \dim \langle e_{1},e_{2}\rangle <\cdots <\dim\langle e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}\rangle\leq n. \]

Hence, at each step the dimension jumps at least by one. Since there are exactly $n$ terms, and the dimension is bounded below by $1$ and bounded above by $n$, the dimension jumps exactly by one at each step, and $\dim\langle e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}\rangle= n $.

We are finally in a position to proof the theorem to the effect that the dimension of a finite dimension vector space agrees with the cardinality of any basis.

Proof of the Theorem (Dimension and cardinality of bases)

The theorem is actually a particular case of Proposition (D) . We know by the previous lecture and the theorem on existence of bases (see the proof) that there exists a basis of $E$ of cardinality $n=\dim E$. Call this basis $e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}$. Let $u_{1},\ldots, u_{m}$ be another basis of $E$. We have to prove that $m=n$. Since both span $E$, we have

\[ \langle u_{1},\ldots, u_{m}\rangle=E=\langle e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}\rangle. \]

Since both families are linearly independent, Proposition (D) guarantees that $m=n$. This concludes the proof.

First consequences

Corollary

Let $E$ be a finite-dimensional vector space with $\dim E=n$. If $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ are linearly independent in $E$, then $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ form a basis of $E$.

Proof

Let $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ be a basis of $E$. Then $\langle u_1,\ldots,u_n\rangle \subseteq E = \langle e_1,\ldots,e_n\rangle$. By Proposition (D), we must have $\langle u_1,\ldots,u_n\rangle=\langle e_1,\ldots,e_n\rangle$, hence $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ span $E$ and form a basis. $\square$

Corollary

Let $E$ be a $\mathbb K$-vector space and $F\subseteq E$ a vector subspace. If $\dim E<\infty$, then $\dim F<\infty$ and $\dim F\le \dim E$. Moreover, $\dim F=\dim E$ if and only if $F=E$.

Proof

First, if $\dim F=\infty$ then $\dim E=\infty$. Indeed, assume $\dim E<\infty$ with $E=\langle e_1,\ldots,e_n\rangle$. If $\dim F=\infty$, we can build inductively an infinite linearly independent family in $F$ by choosing $u_1\in F\setminus\{0\}$, then $u_{m+1}\in F\setminus \langle u_1,\ldots,u_m\rangle$. This contradicts Proposition (D) , since $\langle u_1,\ldots,u_m\rangle\subseteq \langle e_1,\ldots,e_n\rangle$ would force $m\le n$ for all $m$.

Now, with $\dim E=n<\infty$, choose bases $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ of $E$ and $u_1,\ldots,u_m$ of $F$. By Proposition (D) and the theorem on bases and cardinality, $\dim F=m\le n=\dim E$, and equality holds if, and only if, $\langle u_1,\ldots,u_m\rangle=\langle e_1,\ldots,e_n\rangle$, i.e. $F=E$. $\square$
