Lecture 5 : extraction, completion, and the Grassmann formula

We continue the study of bases and dimension in finite-dimensional vector spaces, and extract/complete bases using linear independence and spanning arguments.

Extraction of bases

Proposition

Let $E$ be a $\mathbb K$-vector space and assume $E=\langle e_1,\ldots,e_n\rangle$. Then there exists a subfamily $e_{k_1},\ldots,e_{k_m}$ which is a basis of $E$.

Proof

Consider the chain $\langle e_1\rangle \subseteq \langle e_1,e_2\rangle \subseteq \cdots \subseteq \langle e_1,\ldots,e_n\rangle$. At each step either $e_{k+1}\in\langle e_1,\ldots,e_k\rangle$ (no growth), or $e_{k+1}\notin\langle e_1,\ldots,e_k\rangle$, in which case $\dim\langle e_1,\ldots,e_{k+1}\rangle=\dim\langle e_1,\ldots,e_k\rangle+1$ by Lecture 4 . Keeping precisely those $e_i$ that increase the dimension yields a linearly independent spanning subfamily, hence a basis. $\square$

Completion to a basis

Proposition (Incomplete basis theorem)

Let $E$ be a finite-dimensional vector space and let $e_1,\ldots,e_m\in E$ be linearly independent. Then we can complete this family to a basis $e_1,\ldots,e_m,e_{m+1},\ldots,e_n$ of $E$.

Proof

If $\dim E=m$, by Lecture 4 $e_1,\ldots,e_m$ is already a basis. Otherwise $\langle e_1,\ldots,e_m\rangle\ne E$; choose $e_{m+1}\in E\setminus \langle e_1,\ldots,e_m\rangle$. Then $e_1,\ldots,e_m,e_{m+1}$ are independent and $\dim\langle e_1,\ldots,e_{m+1}\rangle=m+1$. Iterating, we eventually obtain $n=\dim E$ independent vectors, which form a basis by Lecture 4 .

Corollary

Let $E$ be a finite-dimensional vector space and let $F\subset E$ be a vector subspace. Then $F$ admits a complement: there exists a subspace $G\subset E$ such that $E=F\oplus G$.

Proof

Take a basis $e_{1},\ldots, e_{m}$ of $F$ and complete it to a basis $e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}$ of $E$, using the incomplete basis theorem. Define $G=\langle e_{m+1},\ldots, e_{n}\rangle$. Then $F+G=E$ and $F\cap G=\lbrace 0\rbrace$ by Lecture 4 . Therefore, $G$ provides a complement of $F$.

Remark

The incomplete basis theorem and the existence of complements hold in infinite dimension. It requires Zorn's lemma.

Grassmann's formula

Given two vector subspaces of a finite dimensional vector space, Grassmann's formula relates the dimension of the sum and the intersection of the vector subspaces, from their own dimension. This is a powerful tool, which allows in many instances to derive information on one of the constructions from information on the other.
Theorem (Grassmann's formula)

Let $E$ be a finite-dimensional vector space, and $F, G\subseteq E$ two vector subspaces. Then

\[ \dim (F+G)=\dim F +\dim G-\dim (F\cap G). \]

In particular, $\dim (F+G)\leq\dim F+\dim G$ and equality holds if, and only if, $F$ and $G$ form a direct sum.

Proof

Let $e_{1},\ldots, e_{m}$ be a basis of $F\cap G$. In particular, the latter has dimension $m$. We complete it to a basis of $F$, by adding vectors $u_{1},\ldots, u_{n}$. In particular, $F$ has dimension $m+n$. Similarly, we can complete $e_{1},\ldots, e_{m}$ to a basis of $G$, by adding vectors $v_{1},\ldots, v_{\ell}$. In particular $G$ has dimension $m+\ell$.

Now, $F+G=\langle e_{1},\ldots, e_{m}, u_{1},\ldots, u_{n}, v_{1},\ldots, v_{\ell}\rangle$. If we see that the exhibited spanning familu of $F+G$ is linearly independent, then we will have in particular shown that $F+G$ has dimension $m+n+\ell$. This equals $(m+n)+(m+\ell)-m$, which is equivalent to the statement of the theorem.

Let us show that $e_{1},\ldots, e_{m}, u_{1},\ldots, u_{n}, v_{1},\ldots, v_{\ell}$ are linearly independent. Suppose we have a relationship

\[ a_{1}e_{1}+\cdots+a_{m}e_{m}+b_{1}u_{1}+\cdots+b_{n}u_{n}+c_{1}v_{1}+\cdots c_{\ell}v_{\ell}=0. \]

Rearrange this expression, in the form

\[ c_{1}v_{1}+\cdots+c_{\ell}v_{\ell}=-a_{1}e_{1}-\cdots-a_{m}e_{m}-b_{1}u_{1}-\cdots-b_{n}u_{n}. \]

The left hand side of this equality belongs to $G$, while the right hand side belongs to $F$. Hence, both sides belong to $F\cap G$. But the latter is given by $\langle e_{1},\ldots, e_{m}\rangle$. By Lecture 4, we have $\langle e_{1},\ldots, e_{m}\rangle\cap \langle v_{1},\ldots, v_{\ell}\rangle=\lbrace 0\rbrace$. Thereofre, we see that the cofficients $c_{i}$ vanish. Then, using that the vectors on the right hand side are linearly independent, we infer that the coefficients $a_{i}$ and $b_{j}$ vanish as well.

The last claim of the statement is immediate. We just need to observe that in case there is an equality, by the Grassmann formula we necessarily have $\dim (F\cap G)=0$, hence $F\cap G=\lbrace 0\rbrace$.

Examples
  1. Let $E=\mathbb{K}[X]_{\leq 3}$, and define $F$ to be the subspace of polynomials $P$ such that $P(0)=P(1)=0$, and $G$ to be the subspace of polynomials $P$ such that $P(2)=P(3)=0$. Since a polynomial of degree smaller than $3$ has at most $3$ distinct roots, we see that $F\cap G=\lbrace 0\rbrace$. We claim that the inclusion $F\oplus G\subseteq E$ is in fact an equality. Since $E$ is four-dimensional, it will be enough to show that $\dim F\geq 2$ and $\dim G\geq 2$. Indeed, then we will have
    \[ 4\leq \dim (F\oplus G)\leq E=4, \]
    forcing the equality. To show that $\dim F\geq 2$, we observe that the polynomials $X(X-1)$ and $X^{2}(X-1)$ belong to $F$, and they are linearly independent. Hence, $\dim F\geq 2$ by Proposition (D) from Lecture 4. Similarly, $G$ contains the polynomials $(X-2)(X-3)$ and $(X-2)^{2}(X-3)$, and they are linearly independent. Hence $\dim G\geq 2$. We note in passing that we have proven that $X(X-1)$, $X^{2}(X-1)$, $(X-2)(X-3)$, $(X-2)^{2}(X-3)$ is a basis of $\mathbb{K}[X]_{\leq 3}$.
  2. Let $E=\mathbb{K}[X]_{\leq 3}$ and $F$ be as above, and define $H$ as the subspace of polynomials $P$ such that $P(1)=P(2)=0$. Notice that $F\cap G$ is the subspace of polynomials $P$ with $P(0)=P(1)=P(2)=0$. Such a polynomial is necessarily a multiple of $X(X-1)(X-2)$, since it has degree less than $3$. We see that $F\cap G=\langle X(X-1)(X-2)\rangle$, which is one dimensional. As in the previous example, $\dim F=\dim H=4$. By the Grassmann's formula, we infer that $\dim (F+H)=3$.
  3. For the sake of comparison, in the previous example, let us prove that $\dim (F+H)=3$ without using the Grassmann's formula. First of all, since we have the inclusions $F\subseteq F+H\subseteq E$, taking dimensions we find $2\leq\dim(F+H)\leq 4$. It will be enough to show that the inequalities are strict. For this, it will be enough to show that the inclusions of vector subspaces are strict. Suppose, for a contradiction, that $F=F+H$. Then, we would have $H\subseteq F$. But this would mean in particular that the polynomial $(X-1)(X-2)$ belongs to $F$. It would thus be of the form $aX(X-1)+bX^{2}(X-1)$. Evaluating at $0$, we would infer that $2=0$, absurd. Similarly, suppose that we had $E=F+H$. This would in particular imply that the constant polynomial $1$ belongs to $F+H$. Now, every polynomial in $F+H$ vanishes at $x=1$, while the constant polynomial 1 does not vanish.
