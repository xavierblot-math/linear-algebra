Lecture 10 : determinants

Preliminaries on permutations

We gather several definitions and facts on permutations, without details of the proofs.

Definition

Let $S$ be a non-empty set. A permutation on $S$ is a bijective map $\sigma\colon S\to S$.

Lemma/Definition

Let $S$ be a non-empty set. The set of permutations on $S$, endowed with the composition of maps, is a group, with neutral element given by the identity map. It is called the group of permutations on $S$.

Definition/Notation

The symmetric group of order $n$, denote by $S_{n}$, is the group of permutations on $\lbrace 1,\ldots, n\rbrace$. We represent a permutation $\sigma\in S_{n}$ by

\[ \sigma=\left(\begin{array}{cccc} 1 &2 &\cdots &n\\ \sigma(1) &\sigma(2) &\cdots &\sigma(n)\end{array}\right). \]
Remark

A map $\sigma\colon\lbrace 1,\ldots, n\rbrace \to \lbrace 1,\ldots,n\rbrace$ is a permutation if, and only if, $\lbrace\sigma(1),\ldots,\sigma(n)\rbrace=\lbrace 1,\ldots,n\rbrace$. That is, $\sigma$ provides a rearrangement of $1,\ldots, n$.

Lemma

The symmetric group $S_{n}$ is finite of cardinality $n!=n\cdot (n-1)\cdots 2\cdot 1$.

Definition/Notation
  1. A permutation $\sigma\in S_{n}$ is said to be a cycle if there exists distinct $i_{1},\ldots, i_{k}\in\lbrace 1,\ldots, n\rbrace$ with
    \begin{equation*} \begin{split} &\sigma(i_{1})=i_{2}\\ &\sigma(i_{2})=i_{3}\\ &\phantom{\sigma}\vdots\\ &\sigma(i_{k-1})=i_{k}\\ &\sigma(i_{k})=i_{1}\\ &\sigma(j)=j\quad\text{if}\quad j\not\in\lbrace i_{1},\ldots, i_{k}\rbrace. \end{split} \end{equation*}

    We denote such a cycle by

    \[ \sigma=(i_{1}\ i_{2}\ \cdots\ i_{k-1}\ i_{k}). \]

    The set $\lbrace i_{1},\ldots, i_{k}\rbrace$ is called the orbit of $\sigma$.

  2. A transposition is a cycle of the form $\tau=(i\ j)$ for some $i\neq j$. Hence, $\tau(i)=j$, $\tau(j)=i$, and $\tau(k)=k$ if $k\neq i,j$.
Proposition

The following assertions hold:

  1. Any permutation can be decomposed as a composition of cycles with disjoint orbits. Up to order, such decomposition is unique.
  2. Any cyclic permutation can be decomposed as a composition of transpositions.
  3. Any permutation can be decomposed as a composition of transpositions. The parity of the number of transpositions (i.e. even or odd) only depends on the permutation, and not on the decomposition.
Definition
  1. The parity of a permutation is the parity of the number of transpositions in any decomposition as a product of transpositions.
  2. The signature of a permutation $\sigma$ is defined as
    \[ \varepsilon(\sigma)=\begin{cases} +1&\quad\text{if }\sigma\text{ is even}\\ -1 &\quad\text{if }\sigma\text{ is odd}. \end{cases} \]

The fact that the parity is well-defined, translates into the following property of the signature.

Lemma

The signature $\varepsilon\colon S_{n}\to\lbrace \pm 1\rbrace$ is a morphism of groups: it satisfies $\varepsilon(\sigma\circ\tau)=\varepsilon(\sigma)\cdot\varepsilon(\tau)$.

Alternative description of the signature

The signature of a permutation can also be described in terms of the number of inversions . Precisely, let $\sigma\in S_{n}$ be a permutation. We define an inversion in $\sigma$ as a couple $(i,j)$ such that $1\leq i\lt j\leq n$ and $\sigma(i)\gt \sigma(j)$.

For a permutation $\sigma$, denote by $I(\sigma)$ the number of inversions in $\sigma$. Then

\[ \varepsilon(\sigma)=(-1)^{I(\sigma)}. \]
Example

Let $\sigma\in S_{7}$ be given by

\[ \sigma=\left(\begin{array}{ccccccc} 1 &2 &3 &4 &5 &6 &7\\ 3 &4 &7 &6 &1 &2 &5 \end{array} \right). \]
Then we can decompose $\sigma$ as a product of two cycles
\[ \sigma=(1\ 3\ 7\ 5)\circ (2\ 4\ 6). \]
For each cycle, we have the decomposition into product of transpositions given by
\[ (1\ 3\ 7\ 5)=(1\ 3)\circ (3\ 7)\circ (7\ 5),\quad (2\ 4\ 6)=(2\ 4)\circ (4\ 6). \]
As a result, we see that $\sigma$ is the product of 5 transpositions, and it is hence odd. Therefore, $\varepsilon(\sigma)=-1$.

Alternating forms

Definition/Notation
Let $E$ be a $\mathbb{K}$-vector space, and $n\geq 1$ an integer. An alternating $n$-form on $E$ is a map
\[ \theta\colon E\times \underset{(n)}{\ldots}\times E\to\mathbb{K} \]
satisfying:
  1. Multilinearity: the map $\theta$ is linear in every variable:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{split} \theta(e_{1},\ldots, e_{k-1},ae_{k}+be_{k}^{\prime},e_{k+1},\ldots, e_{n})=&a\ \theta(e_{1},\ldots, e_{k-1},e_{k},e_{k+1},\ldots, e_{n})\\ &+b\ \theta(e_{1},\ldots, e_{k-1},e_{k}^{\prime},e_{k+1},\ldots, e_{n}). \end{split} \end{equation*}
  2. It is alternating: if $x_{i}=x_{j}$ for some $i\neq j$, then $\theta(x_{1},\ldots, x_{n})=0$.

The set of alternating $n$-forms on $E$ is denoted by $\mathcal{A}_{n}(E)$. Pointwise additions and multiplication by scalars endow it with a natural structure of vector space over $\mathbb{K}$.

Lemma

Let $\theta$ be an alternating $n$-form on $E$, and $\sigma\in S_{n}$ a permutation. Then $\theta$ satisfies

\[ \theta(e_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,e_{\sigma(n)})=\varepsilon(\sigma)\theta(e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}). \]

In particular, if $1\leq i\lt j\leq n$, then

\[ \theta(e_{1},\ldots, \underset{(i)}{e_{i}},\ldots, \underset{(j)}{e_{j}},\ldots, e_{n})=-\theta(e_{1},\ldots, \underset{(i)}{e_{j}},\ldots, \underset{(j)}{e_{i}},\ldots, e_{n}). \]
Proof

Every permutation decomposes into a product of transpositions, and $\varepsilon$ is a morphism of groups. From this, we can reduce to the second claim in the statement.

For the second claim, since $\theta$ is alternating, we have

\[ \theta(e_{1},\ldots,\underset{(i)}{e_{i}+e_{j}},\ldots,\underset{(j)}{e_{i}+e_{j}},\ldots, e_{n})=0. \]

Using the multilinearity and the alternating property again, this is equivalent to

\[ \theta(e_{1},\ldots, \underset{(i)}{e_{i}},\ldots, \underset{(j)}{e_{j}},\ldots, e_{n})+\theta(e_{1},\ldots, \underset{(i)}{e_{j}},\ldots, \underset{(j)}{e_{i}},\ldots, e_{n})=0. \]

The following is a key statement on the structure of the spaces of alternating forms on a finite dimensional vector space, leading to the theory of determinants.

Theorem
Let $E$ be a finite dimensional $\mathbb{K}$-vector space, of dimension $n\geq 1$. Then:
  1. The space $\mathcal{A}_{n}(E)$ is one-dimensional.
  2. If $\mathcal{B}=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})$ is a basis of $E$, then there is an associated basis $\det_{\mathcal{B}}$ of $\mathcal{A}_{n}(E)$, characterized by the normalization property $\det_{\mathcal{B}}(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})=1$.
Proof

Let $\theta\in\mathcal{A}_{n}(E)$. Let $\mathcal{B}=(e_{1},\ldots, e_{n})$ be a basis of $E$. Given $u_{1},\ldots, u_{n}\in E$, write

\[ u_{j}=a_{1j}e_{1}+\ldots+a_{nj}e_{n}. \]

Then, by the multilinearity of $\theta$ we have

\[ \theta(u_{1},\ldots, u_{n})=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots, i_{n}}a_{i_{1}1}\cdots a_{i_{n}n}\theta(e_{i_{1}},\ldots, e_{i_{n}}). \]

A priori, the sum runs over all the possible indices $i_{1},\ldots, i_{n}$. However, since $\theta$ is alternating, the contributions with repeated indices vanish. Hence, we can assume that the indices $i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}$ are all distinct. These thus uniquely define a permutation $\sigma$, by $\sigma(k)=i_{k}$. And any permutation defines such a set of distinct indices. We may thus rewrite

\[ \theta(u_{1},\ldots, u_{n})=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}a_{\sigma(1),1}\cdots a_{\sigma(n),n}\cdot \theta(e_{\sigma(1)},\ldots, e_{\sigma(n)}). \]

Finally, by the previous proposition, we conclude

\[ \theta(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})=\left(\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\varepsilon(\sigma)a_{\sigma(1),1}\cdots a_{\sigma(n),n}\right)\theta(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}). \]

This motivates the following definition:

\[ \mathrm{det}_{\mathcal{B}}(u_{1},\ldots, u_{n})=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\varepsilon(\sigma)a_{\sigma(1),1}\cdots a_{\sigma(n),n}. \]

From the properties of the signature, one can easily check that this is an alternating $n$-form, and by construction $\det_{\mathcal{B}}(e_{1},\ldots, e_{n})=1$, so it is non-zero. The above computation shows that

\[ \theta=\lambda\cdot\mathrm{det}_{\mathcal{B}}, \]

where $\lambda=\theta(e_{1},\ldots, e_{n})$. Therefore, we have proven that $\det_{\mathcal{B}}$ spans $\mathcal{A}_{n}(E)$, and it is non-zero.

Determinants of endomorphisms and matrices

The structure theorem on the space of alternating $n$-forms is the key point of the following construction.
Determinant of an endomorphism

Let $E$ be a finite dimensional vector space, of dimension $n\geq 1$. Let $\varphi\colon E\to E$ be an endomorphism. Then, if $\theta$ is an alternating $n$-form, we define

\[ \varphi^{\ast}(\theta)(u_{1},\ldots, u_{n})=\theta(\varphi(u_{1}),\ldots, \varphi(u_{n})). \]

This is an alternating $n$-form on $E$, and the construction actually produces an endomorphism

\[ \varphi^{\ast}\colon \mathcal{A}_{n}(E)\to\mathcal{A}_{n}(E). \]

We know that $\mathcal{A}_{n}(E)$ is one-dimensional, and this implies that there exists a scalar $\lambda$ such that

\[ \varphi^{\ast}(\theta)=\lambda\cdot\theta, \]

for every $\theta\in\mathcal{A}_{n}(E)$. Concretely, fixing a basis $\mathcal{B}=(e_{1},\ldots, e_{n})$ of $E$, we will have

\[ \lambda=\mathrm{det}_{\mathcal{B}}(\varphi(e_{1}),\ldots,\varphi(e_{n})). \]

Even more concretely, if $A=(a_{ij})$ is the matrix of $\varphi$ in the basis $\mathcal{B}$, we will have

\[ \lambda=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\varepsilon(\sigma)a_{\sigma(1),1}\cdots a_{\sigma(n),n}. \]

We call this quantity the determinant of the endomorphism $\varphi$, and we denote it by $\det\varphi$.

The determinant of a matrix is a particular case of the determinant of an endomorphism.

Determinant of a matrix

Let $A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{K})$ be a square matrix. We define the determinant of $A$, denoted by $\det A$ or $|A|$, as the determinant of $A$ interpreted as en endomorphism $A\colon\mathbb{K}^{n}\to\mathbb{K}^{n}$.

If $A=(a_{ij})=(C_{1}\ \cdots\ C_{n})$, where $C_{i}\in\mathbb{K}^{n}$ are columns vectors, then

\[ \det A=\det(C_{1},\ldots, C_{n})=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\varepsilon(\sigma)a_{\sigma(1),1}\cdots a_{\sigma(n),n}. \]

Here, the notation $\det$ refers to the determinant with respect to the standard basis of $\mathbb{K}^{n}$.

Note that, reordering the terms in the product $a_{\sigma(1),1}\cdots a_{\sigma(n),n}$, we can rewrite

\[ a_{\sigma(1),1}\cdots a_{\sigma(n),n}=a_{1,\sigma^{-1}(1)}\cdots a_{n,\sigma^{-1}(n)}. \]

This is an equality for the product, and not term by term! We also observe that $\varepsilon(\sigma^{-1})=\varepsilon(\sigma)^{-1}=\varepsilon(\sigma)$, since the latter takes values $\pm 1$. From this, we find

\begin{equation*} \begin{split} \sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\varepsilon(\sigma)a_{\sigma(1),1}\cdots a_{\sigma(n),n}=&\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\varepsilon(\sigma)a_{1,\sigma^{-1}(1)}\cdots a_{n,\sigma^{-1}(n)}\\ =&\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\varepsilon(\sigma^{-1})a_{1,\sigma^{-1}(1)}\cdots a_{n,\sigma^{-1}(n)}\\ =&\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\varepsilon(\sigma)a_{1,\sigma(1)}\cdots a_{n,\sigma(n)}, \end{split} \end{equation*}

where we use the fact that if $\sigma$ runs over the elements $S_{n}$, then $\sigma^{-1}$ runs over the element of $S_{n}$ too. The previous equality means that the determinant of $A=(a_{ij})_{ij}$ is the same thing as the determinant of its transpose $^{t}A=(a_{ji})_{ij}$:

\[ \det A=\det(^{t} A). \]
Proposition

Let $E$ be a finite dimensional vector space.

  1. If $\varphi,\psi\colon E\to E$ are endomorphisms, then $\det(\varphi\circ\psi)=\det(\varphi)\cdot\det(\psi)$.
  2. If $\varphi\colon E\to E$ is an endomorphism, then $\varphi$ is invertible if, and only if, $\det\varphi\neq 0$. Moreover, in this case, $\det(\varphi^{-1})=(\det\varphi)^{-1}$
Proof

For the first claim, we have, on the one hand

\[ (\varphi\circ\psi)^{\ast}(\theta)=\det(\varphi\circ\psi)\theta. \]
On the other hand, one easily checks $(\varphi\circ\psi)^{\ast}=\psi^{\ast}\circ\varphi^{\ast}$, so that
\[ (\varphi\circ\psi)^{\ast}(\theta)=\psi^{\ast}(\varphi^{\ast}(\theta))=(\det\psi)(\det\varphi)\theta. \]

Next, if $\varphi$ is invertible, then let $\varphi^{-1}$ be an inverse. Then, by the property above,

\[ 1=\det(\mathrm{id}_{E})=(\det\varphi)(\det(\varphi^{-1})). \]

Hence, the $\det\varphi\neq 0$ and $\det(\varphi^{-1})=(\det\varphi)^{-1}$.

Conversely, suppose that $\varphi$ is not invertible. Fix a basis $\mathcal{B}=(e_{1},\ldots, e_{n})$ of $E$. Define $u_{i}=\varphi(e_{i})$. Because $\varphi$ is an endomorphism of a finite dimensional space, and it is not invertible by assumption, we infer that $u_{1},\ldots, u_{n}$ are not linearly independent. Possibly reordering the basis, we can suppose that there is a linear relationship

\[ u_{1}=\lambda_{2}u_{2}+\cdots+\lambda_{n}u_{n}. \]

Then

\[ \det\varphi=\mathrm{det}_{\mathcal{B}}(u_{1},\ldots, u_{n})=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\lambda_{i}\mathrm{\det}_{\mathcal{B}}(u_{i},u_{2},\ldots, u_{n}). \]

By the alternating property, the right hand side vanishes, since there are repeated entries.

We gather the properties of the determinant of a matrix, resulting from the theory discussed above.

Properties of the determinant

For column vectors $C_{1},\ldots, C_{n}\in \mathbb{K}^{n}$, let $\det(C_{1},\ldots, C_{n})$ be the determinant of the matrix $(C_{1}\ \cdots\ C_{n})$.

  1. Multilinearity.
    \[ \det(C_{1},\ldots, C_{i-1}, \sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}C_{i}^{(k)},C_{i+1},\ldots,C_{n})=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\det(C_{1},\ldots, C_{i-1},C_{i}^{(k)},C_{i+1},\ldots, C_{n}). \]
    In particular, for $A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{K})$ and $\lambda\in\mathbb{K}$, $\det(\lambda\cdot A)=\lambda^{n}\det A$.
  2. Alternating. For indices $i\lt j$, if $C_{i}=C_{j}$, then $\det(C_{1},\ldots, C_{n})=0$.
  3. Alternating (variant). For indices $i\lt j$, we have
    \[ \det(C_{1},\ldots, \underset{(i)}{C_{i}},\ldots, \underset{(j)}{C_{j}},\ldots, e_{n})=-\det(C_{1},\ldots, \underset{(i)}{C_{j}},\ldots, \underset{(j)}{C_{i}},\ldots, C_{n}). \]
  4. As a result of the previous properties, if $C_{i}^{\prime}=C_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\lambda_{j}C_{j}$, then
    5. \[ \det(C_{1},\ldots, C_{i-1},C_{i}^{\prime},C_{i+1},\ldots, C_{n})=\det(C_{1},\ldots, C_{i-1},C_{i},C_{i+1},\ldots, C_{n}). \]
  5. Multiplicativity. For matrices $A,B\in M_{n}(\mathbb{K})$, $\det(A\cdot B)=(\det A)\cdot (\det B)$.
  6. Determinant and inverse. A matrix $A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{K})$ is invertible if, and only if, $\det A\neq 0$. Moreover, in this case, $\det(A^{-1})=(\det A)^{-1}$.
  7. Determinant and linear independence. The vectors $C_{1},\ldots, C_{n}\in\mathbb{K}^{n}$ are linearly independent if, and only if, $\det(C_{1},\ldots, C_{n})\neq 0$.
  8. Determinant and transpose. If $A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{K})$, then $\det A=\det(^{t}A)$.
  9. As a result of the previous two properties, the columns of a square matrix are linearly independent if, and only if, the rows are linearly independent.

Inductive computation of determinants and inverses

Definition/Notation

Let $A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{K})$ be a square matrix.

  1. We denote by $A_{ij}\in M_{n-1}(\mathbb{K})$ the matrix obtained from $A$ by deleting the $i$-th row and the $j$-th column.
  2. We denote by $a_{ij}^{\ast}=(-1)^{i+j}\det A_{ij}$.
  3. We define the comatrix of $A$ by $\mathrm{com}\ A=(a_{ij}^{\ast})$.

The following statement provides a rule to inductively compute the determinant of a matrix from smaller determinants. The proof is not difficult, but it is tedious and not specially illuminating, and we omit it.

Proposition

Let $A=(a_{ij})\in M_{n}(\mathbb{K})$, and fix a row $i_{0}$ and a column $j_{0}$. Then

\[ \det A=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{i_{0}j}\cdot a_{i_{0}j}^{\ast}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ij_{0}}\cdot a_{ij_{0}}^{\ast}. \]

Another tedious computation leads to the following corollary, whose proof we also omit. It provides a general expressions for the inverse of a matrix, if it exists.

Corollary

For a square matrix $A$,

\[ A\cdot \mathrm{com}({^{t}A})=\mathrm{com}({^{t}A})\cdot A=(\det A)\cdot I_{n}. \]

In particular, if $A$ is invertible, then

\[ A^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det A}\mathrm{com}(^{t}A). \]
Remark

One has the equality $\mathrm{com}(^{t}A)= {^{t}\mathrm{com}\ A}$.

Let us consider the matrix

\[ A=\left(\begin{array}{ccc} 1 &-1 &0\\ 0 &1 &1\\ 1 &2 &1\end{array}\right). \]

Expanding along the first column, we find

\begin{equation*} \begin{split} \det A=\left |\begin{array}{ccc} 1 &-1 &0\\ 0 &1 &1\\ 1 &2 &1\end{array}\right|=&1\cdot \left|\begin{array}{cc}1 &1\\ 2 &1\end{array}\right| - 0\cdot \left|\begin{array}{cc}-1 &0\\ 2 &1\end{array}\right|+1\cdot \left|\begin{array}{cc}-1 &0\\ 1 &1\end{array}\right|\\ =&1\cdot (1-2)+1\cdot (-1)=-2. \end{split} \end{equation*}

The comatrix is given by

\begin{equation*} \begin{split} \mathrm{com}\ A&=\left(\begin{array}{ccc} \left|\begin{array}{cc} 1 & 1\\ 2 &1\end{array}\right| &-\left|\begin{array}{cc} 0 & 1\\ 1 &1\end{array}\right| &\left|\begin{array}{cc} 0 & 1\\ 1 &2\end{array}\right|\\ -\left|\begin{array}{cc} -1 & 0\\ 2 &1\end{array}\right| &\left|\begin{array}{cc} 1 & 0\\ 1 &1\end{array}\right| &-\left|\begin{array}{cc} 1 & -1\\ 1 &2\end{array}\right| \\ \left|\begin{array}{cc} -1 & 0\\ 1 &1\end{array}\right| &-\left|\begin{array}{cc} 1 & 0\\ 0 &1\end{array}\right| &\left|\begin{array}{cc} 1 & -1\\ 0 &1\end{array}\right|\end{array} \right)\\ &=\left(\begin{array}{ccc} -1 &1 &-1\\ 1 &1 &-3\\ -1 &-1 &1 \end{array} \right). \end{split} \end{equation*}

We thus see that $A$ is invertible with inverse

\[ A^{-1}=-\frac{1}{2}{^{t}\mathrm{com}\ A}=\left(\begin{array}{ccc}1/2 &-1/2 &1/2\\ -1/2 &-1/2 &1/2\\ 1/2 &3/2 &-1/2 \end{array}\right). \]

This coincides with the inverse computed by the Gauss-type method in Lecture 8.
