Lecture 8 : spaces of linear maps and matrices

Spaces of linear maps and matrices

Let $E$ and $F$ be vector spaces over $\mathbb{K}$. We assume in this lecture that these are finite dimensional. We saw in Lecture 6 that the space of linear maps from $E$ to $F$, denoted by $\mathcal{L}(E,F)$ or $\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{K}}(E,F)$, is naturally a vector space over $\mathbb{K}$: the vector space structure of $F$ induces a vector space structure on $\mathcal{L}(E,F)$.

Construction

Let $e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}$ be a basis of $E$ and $u_{1},\ldots, u_{m}$ be a basis of $F$. Then, by Lecture 6 there exist unique linear maps

\[ \varphi\colon E\to\mathbb{K}^{n}\quad\text{and}\quad \psi\colon F\to\mathbb{K}^{m}, \]

characterized by sending the $i$-th basis vector to the $i$-th standard basis element. Moreover, these are necessarily bijective. Therefore, given a linear map $f\colon E\to F$, we can construct a new linear map $\mathbb{K}^{n}\to\mathbb{K}^{m}$ by

\[ \mathbb{K}^{n}\overset{\varphi^{-1}}{\longrightarrow} E\overset{f}{\longrightarrow} F\overset{\psi}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{K}^{m}, \]

namely $\varphi^{-1}\circ f\circ \psi$. Conversely, given a linear map $g\colon\mathbb{K}^{n}\to\mathbb{K}^{m}$, we can form

\[ E\overset{\varphi}{\longrightarrow} \mathbb{K}^{n}\overset{g}{\longrightarrow} \mathbb{K}^{m}\overset{\psi^{-1}}{\longrightarrow} F. \]
Proposition

With the notation as above, there is an isomorphism of $\mathbb{K}$-vector spaces

\begin{equation*} \begin{split} \mathcal{L}(E,F)&\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{K}^{n},\mathbb{K}^{m})\\ f &\longmapsto \psi\circ f\circ \varphi^{-1}, \end{split} \end{equation*}

with inverse

\begin{equation*} \begin{split} \mathcal{L}(\mathbb{K}^{n},\mathbb{K}^{m})&\longrightarrow\mathcal{L}(E,F)\\ g &\longmapsto \psi^{-1}\circ g\circ \varphi. \end{split} \end{equation*}
Proof
The constructions are clearly inverse to each other, and easily seen to be linear in $f$ and $g$.

We next consider the relationship with the formalism of matrices.

Definition/Notation

We denote by $M_{mn}(\mathbb{K})$ the set of matrices with $m$ rows and $n$ columns (resp. $M_{m}(\mathbb{K})$ if $m=n$). It is seen as a vector space over $\mathbb{K}$, with componentwise addition and multiplication by scalars.

The zero matrix, denoted by $0$, is the matrix all of whose entries are $0$.

The identity matrix of size $m$ is the matrix $I_{m}$ with $1$ on the diagonal and $0$ otherewise. It is sometimes more simply denoted by $1_{m}$ or $1$.

Proposition

For a matrix $A\in M_{mn}(\mathbb{K})$, denote by $f_{A}\colon \mathbb{K}^{n}\to\mathbb{K}^{m}$ denote the associated linear map. The, there is a bijective linear map

\begin{equation*} \begin{split} M_{mn}(\mathbb{K})&\longrightarrow \mathcal{L}(\mathbb{K}^{n},\mathbb{K}^{m})\\ A &\longmapsto f_{A}, \end{split} \end{equation*}

with inverse given

\begin{equation*} \begin{split} \mathcal{L}(\mathbb{K}^{n},\mathbb{K}^{m}) &\longrightarrow M_{mn}(\mathbb{K})\\ f &\longmapsto A_{f} \end{split} \end{equation*}

where $A_{f}=(f(e_{1})\ \cdots \ f(e_{n})$ is the matrix obtained from the column vectors $f(e_{i})$, image of the standard basis vectors $e_{i}$ under $f$.

Proof

Both constructions are linear. For instance, the characterization of the construction $A\mapsto f_{A}$ readily implies the linearity in $A$. For $f\mapsto A_{f}$, this is apparent from the very definition. They are easily seen to be mutual inverse constructions.

Combining the previous results, we derive:
Corollary

Let $E$, $F$ be finite dimensional vector spaces over $\mathbb{K}$. Fix bases $e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}$ of $E$ and $u_{1},\ldots, u_{m}$ of $F$. Given $f\in\mathcal{L}(E,F)$, denote by $M_{f}$ the matrix

\[ M_{f}=\left(\begin{array}{ccc} a_{11} &\cdots &a_{1n}\\ \vdots & &\vdots\\ a_{m1} &\cdots & a_{mn} \end{array} \right) \]

determined by

\begin{equation*} \begin{split} f(e_{1})&=a_{11}u_{1}+\cdots + a_{m1}u_{m}\\ &\vdots \\ f(e_{n})&=a_{1n} u_{1}+\cdots+ a_{mn} u_{m}. \end{split} \end{equation*}

Then, there is a bijective linear map

\begin{equation*} \begin{split} \mathcal{L}(E,F) &\longrightarrow M_{mn}(\mathbb{K})\\ f &\longmapsto M_{f}. \end{split} \end{equation*}
Definition

With the notation as in the previous corollary, we say that $M_{f}$ is the matrix of $f$ in the bases $(e_{i})_{i}$ and $(u_{j})_j$.

Remark

It is important to note that, while the definition of basis does not require any order, the construction of the matrix of a linear map is naturally associated to an orderer basis, and the result depends of the order. Also, it is sometimes better to include the bases in the notation: if $\mathcal{B}$ refers to the basis $(e_{i})_{i}$ and $\mathcal{B}^{\prime}$ depends on the basis $(u_{j})_{j}$, then we may denote by $\mathrm{Mat}_{f,\mathcal{B},\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}$ the matrix of $f$ in these bases.

Example

  1. In $\mathbb{K}[X]_{\leq 3}$, fix the basis $1, X, X^{2}, X^{3}$. Consider the linear map
    \begin{equation*} \begin{split} D\colon \mathbb{K}[X]_{\leq 3}&\longrightarrow \mathbb{K}[X]_{\leq 3}\\ P(X) &\longmapsto P'(X). \end{split} \end{equation*}
    Then, the matrix of $D$ in the basis above is
    \[ M_{D}=\left( \begin{array}{cccc} 0 &1 &0 &0\\ 0 &0 &2 &0\\ 0 &0 &0 &3\\ 0 &0 &0 &0 \end{array} \right). \]
  2. For $a\in\mathbb{K}$, introduce the evaluation map $\mathrm{ev}_{a}\colon\mathbb{K}[X]_{\leq d}\to\mathbb{K}$, given by $\mathrm{ev}_{a}(P(X))=P(a)$. If we take the basis $1, X, \ldots, X^{d}$ of $\mathbb{K}[X]_{\leq d}$ and $1$ of $\mathbb{K}$, then the matrix associated to $\mathrm{ev}_{a}$ is the vector
    \[ M_{\mathrm{ev}_{a}}=(1, a, a^{2},\ldots, a^{d}). \]
    If instead we consider the basis $1, (X-a), (X-a)^{2},\ldots, (X-a)^{d}$ of $\mathbb{K}[X]_{\leq d}$, then the associated matrix is
    \[ M_{\mathrm{ev}_{a}}'=(1, 0, 0,\ldots, 0). \]
Corollary

Let $E, F$ be finite dimensional vector spaces over $\mathbb{K}$. Then $\dim\mathcal{L}(E,F)=(\dim E)(\dim F)$. In particular, $\dim M_{mn}(\mathbb{K})=mn$.

Proof
It is enough to prove the statement for the space of matrices. Indeed, if we choose bases of $E$ and $F$, then we have an associated bijective linear map $\mathcal{L}(E,F)\simeq M_{mn}(\mathbb{K})$ given by the matrix of a linear map in the given bases. For the space of matrices, define $\delta_{ij}$ as the matrix with $1$ at the position $ij$ and $0$ elsewhere. This is easily seen to be a basis of $M_{mn}(\mathbb{K})$. This basis has cardinality $mn$.

Matrix multiplication and invertible matrices

We have established the relationship between matrices and linear maps. This indicated that the composition of linear maps corresponds to some matrix operation. This operation is matrix multiplication.

Definition (matrix multiplication)

Let $A=(a_{ij})_{ij}\in M_{\ell m}(\mathbb{K})$ and $B=(b_{jk})\in M_{mn}(\mathbb{K})$ be matrices, so that in particular the number of rows of $B$ is equal to the number of columns of $A$, equal to $m$. We define the product $A\cdot B$, or simply $AB$, as the matrix $C=(c_{ik})_{ik}$ in $M_{\ell n}(\mathbb{K})$, given by

\[ c_{ik}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{ij}b_{jk}. \]

That is, the entry $c_{ik}$ at the position $ik$ of $C$ (row $i$, column $k$), is given by: take the $k$-th column of $B$, turn it into a horizontal vector, and hit it against the $i$-th row of $A$, namely multiply their entries and take the sum of the result.

Remark

The following are easily checked:

  • Product with the $0$ matrix. $A\cdot 0=0$ and $0\cdot A=0$.
  • Product with the identity matrix. For $A\in M_{mn}(\mathbb{K})$, we have $A\cdot I_{m}=A$, and $I_{n}\cdot A=A$.
  • Associativity. Whenever defined, $(A\cdot B)\cdot C=A\cdot (B\cdot C)$.
  • Distributivity. Whenever defined, $A\cdot (B+C)=A\cdot B+A\cdot C$, and similarly $(A+B)\cdot C=A\cdot C+B\cdot C$.

The precise relationship between composition of linear maps and matrix multiplication is described below.

Lemma

Let $E, F, G$ be finite dimensional vector spaces, with respective bases $e_{1},\ldots e_{n}$, $u_{1},\ldots, u_{m}$ and $v_{1},\ldots, v_{\ell}$. Let $f\colon F\to G$ and $g\colon E\to F$ be linear maps, with matrices $A$ and $B$ in the given bases. Then, the linear map $f\circ g\colon E\to G$ has matrix $A\cdot B$ in the given bases.

Proof

Write

\[ g(e_{k})=\sum_{j=1}^{m} b_{jk}u_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad f(u_{j})=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}a_{ij}v_{i}. \]

Using the linearity of $f$ and its action on the given basis, we have

\[ (f\circ g)(e_{k})=f\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m} b_{jk}u_{j}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{jk}f(u_{j})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{jk}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}a_{ij}v_{i}. \]

Rearranging this expression, we obtain

\[ (f\circ g)(e_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{ij}b_{jk}\right)v_{i}. \]
Once the relationship between composition of linear maps and matrix multiplication is clear, we can discuss invertibility of matrices.
Definition (invertibility for matrices)

Let $A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{K})$. We say that $A$ is invertible if, and only if, the associated linear map $f_{A}$ is bijective. In this case, we denote by $A^{-1}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{K})$ the matrix such that $(f_{A})^{-1}=f_{A^{-1}}$.

Remark

By the relationship between matrix multiplication and composition of linear maps, we see that for an invertible matrix $A$ we have $A\cdot A^{-1}=A^{-1}\cdot A=I_{n}$.

Lemma

Let $A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{K})$. The following are equivalent:

  1. $A$ is invertible.
  2. $\mathrm{rk} A=n$.
  3. $\mathrm{nul} A=0$.
  4. There exists $B\in M_{n}(\mathbb{K})$ such that $A\cdot B=I_{n}$.
  5. There exists $B\in M_{n}(\mathbb{K})$ such that $B\cdot A=I_{n}$.
  6. For every $Y\in\mathbb{K}^{n}$, the equation $A\cdot X=Y$ has a unique solution $X\in\mathbb{K}^{n}$.

Moreover, if these conditions are satisfied, then (d) and (e) $B=A^{-1}$.

Proof

The equivalence of (a), (b), (c) follows from the corresponding property for the associated endomorphism $f_{A}$: equivalence between bijectivity, surjectivity and injectivity. If we have (d), then $f_{A}\circ f_{B}=\mathrm{id}$, which entails that $f_{A}$ is surjective, hence $\mathrm{rk} A=n$. If we have (e), then $f_{B}\circ f_{A}=\mathrm{if}$, which entails that $f_{A}$ is injective, hence $\mathrm{nul} A=0$. Property (f) amounts to saying that $f_{A}$ is bijective. Finally, if $A$ is invertible, and if $A\cdot B=I_{n}$, then we can multiply the equality from the left by $A^{-1}$, and using the associativity of the product we derive that $B=A^{-1}$. Similarly of $B\cdot A=I_{n}$.

Gaussian algorithm for the computation of the inverse

In practice, to find the inverse of a matrix $A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{K})$, we consider the equation $A\cdot B=I_{n}$, which needs to be solved for $B$. Wrtie $B=(X_{1}\ \cdots\ X_{n})$, where the $X_{i}$ are unknown column vectors in $\mathbb{K}^{n}$. Then

\[ A\cdot B=(A\cdot X_{1}\ \cdots\ A\cdot X_{n}). \]

Then, $A\cdot B=I_{n}$ amounts to solving $n$ equations:

\begin{equation*} \begin{split} A\cdot X_{1} &=\left( \begin{array}{c} 1\\ 0\\ \vdots\\ 0\end{array}\right)\\ &\vdots\\ A\cdot X_{n}&=\left( \begin{array}{c} 0\\ 0\\ \vdots\\ 1\end{array}\right). \end{split} \end{equation*}

This can be addressed by a Gauss-type elimination algorithm. One forms an augmented matrix $(A\mid I_{n})$. Hence, on the left hand side we write the matrix $A$, and on the right hand side we write the identity matrix $I_{n}$. Then, we perform the usual Gauss-type algorithm on rows: exchanging rows, mutliplying rows by non-zero scalars, adding to a row another row. One has to keep track of these operations on both sides of the augmented matrix. Step by step, one tries to carry the augmented matrix $(A\mid I_{n})$ into the form $(I_{n}\mid B)$. If this process succeeds, then $B=A^{-1}$. Note that this works exactly when $A$ is invertible.

Example

Let us work out an example of the Gaussian-type algorithm to determine the inverse of a matrix. Define

\[ A=\left(\begin{array}{ccc} 1 &-1 &0\\ 0 &1 &1\\ 1 &2 &1\end{array}\right). \]

We form the augmented matrix:

\[ \widetilde{A}= \left(\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 &-1 &0 &1 &0 &0\\ 0 &1 &1 &0 &1 &0\\ 1 &2 &1 &0 &0 &1\end{array}\right). \]

We denote by $R_{1}$, $R_{2}$, $R_{3}$ the rows of $\widetilde{A}$. We next perform the allowed operations on rows, in order to achieve the goal: producing the identity matrix on the left hand side. The resulting matrix on the right hand side will then be $A^{-1}$.

  • $R_{3}\leftarrow R_{3}-R_{1}$. By this we mean that we replace the row $R_{3}$ by $R_{3}-R_{1}$. The new row thus obtained is still denoted by $R_{3}$. The result of this manipulation is
    \[ \left(\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 &-1 &0 &1 &0 &0\\ 0 &1 &1 &0 &1 &0\\ 0 &3 &1 &-1 &0 &1\end{array}\right). \]
  • $R_{3}\leftarrow R_{3}-R_{2}$
    \[ \left(\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 &-1 &0 &1 &0 &0\\ 0 &1 &1 &0 &1 &0\\ 0 &2 &0 &-1 &-1 &1\end{array}\right). \]
  • $R_{3}\leftrightarrow R_{2}$. By this we mean exchanging the roles of $R_{3}$ and $R_{2}$. The result is
    \[ \left(\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 &-1 &0 &1 &0 &0\\ 0 &2 &0 &-1 &-1 &1\\ 0 &1 &1 &0 &1 &0\end{array}\right). \]
  • $R_{1}\leftarrow 2R_{1}$ and $R_{3}\leftarrow 2R_{3}$. Alternatively, one could divide $R_{2}$ by $2$, but this would have the effect of introducing denominators and making computations unpleasant. We thus try to postpone the introduction of denominators until the end. The result is
    \[ \left(\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 2 &-2 &0 &2 &0 &0\\ 0 &2 &0 &-1 &-1 &1\\ 0 &2 &2 &0 &2 &0\end{array}\right). \]
  • $R_{1}\leftarrow R_{1}+R_{2}$ and $R_{3}\leftarrow R_{3}-R_{2}$.
    \[ \left(\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 2 &0 &0 &1 &-1 &1\\ 0 &2 &0 &-1 &-1 &1\\ 0 &0 &2 &1 &3 &-1\end{array}\right). \]
  • Finally we divide by $2$, and we obtain
    \[ \left(\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 &0 &0 &1/2 &-1/2 &1/2\\ 0 &1 &0 &-1/2 &-1/2 &1/2\\ 0 &0 &1 &1/2 &3/2 &-1/2\end{array}\right). \]

We conclude that the inverse of $A$ indeed exists and is given by

\[ A^{-1}=\left(\begin{array}{ccc} 1/2 &-1/2 &1/2\\ -1/2 &-1/2 &1/2\\ 1/2 &3/2 &-1/2\end{array}\right). \]

After such a computation, it is wise to check the result. In this case, we see that indeed $A\cdot A^{-1}=I_{3}$.

Linear maps, matrices and change of bases

We have seen that, upon choosing bases , linear maps between finite dimensional vector spaces are in a linear bijective correspondance with matrices. We next elaborate on the dependency of the choice of matrix.

Change of bases (or change of coordinates) matrices

Let $E$ and $F$ be finite dimensional vector spaces over $\mathbb{K}$. Suppose we are given bases $(e_{1},\ldots, e_{n})$ and $(e_{1}',\ldots, e_{n}')$ of $E$, and $(u_{1},\ldots, u_{m})$ and $(u_{1}',\ldots, u_{m}')$ of $F$.

Let $f\colon E\to F$ be a $\mathbb{K}$-linear map. We denote by $M_{f}$ the matrix of $f$ in the bases $(e_{i})$ and $(u_{j})$. Similarly, we denote by $M_{f}'$ the matrix of $f$ in the bases $(e_{i}')$ and $(u_{j}')$.

The relationship between $M_{f}'$ and $M_{f}$ is obtained by writing the tautological factorization

\[ f=\mathrm{id}_{F}\circ f\circ \mathrm{id}_{E}. \]

Define:

  • $B$ the matrix of $\mathrm{id}_{E}\colon E\to E$, in the bases $(e_{i}')$ and $(e_{i})$. Hence, $B$ is obtained by writing
    \[ e_{i}'=b_{1i} e_{1} + b_{2i}e_{2}+\cdots b_{ni} e_{n}, \]
    and organizing the coefficients $b_{1i},\ldots, b_{ni}$ as the columns of $B$. The matrix $B$ passes from the basis $(e_{i}')$ to the basis $(e_{i})$. Precisely, if $u\in E$ and $(a_{1},\ldots, a_{n})$ are its coordinates in the basis $e_{i}'$, then
    \[ B\cdot\left(\begin{array}{c} a_{1}\\ \vdots\\ a_{n}\end{array}\right) \]
    provides the coordinates of $u$ in the basis $(e_{i})$. You can double-check this by considering $u=e_{i}'$, in which case $a_{i}=1$ and $a_{j}=0$ for $j\neq i$. Then you recover the coefficients $(b_{1},\ldots, b_{ni})$.
  • $A$ the matrix of $\mathrm{id}_{F}\colon F\to F$, in the bases $(u_{j})$ and $(u_{j}')$. Hence, one write the bases vectors $u_{j}$ in the new basis $(u_{j}')$, and organizes the resulting coefficients as the columns of the matrix $A$.

Then, the relationship $f=\mathrm{id}_{F}\circ f\circ \mathrm{id}_{E}$ translates into

\[ M_{f}'=A\cdot M_{f}\cdot B. \]

In a diagram:

A particular important case is when $E=F$, that is $f\colon E\to E$ is an endomorphism. Then, in the procedure above, $A=B^{-1}$ is the matrix which passes from the basis $(e_{i})$ to the basis $(e_{i}')$. The relationship between $M_{f}$ and $M_{f}'$ is then

\[ M_{f}'=B^{-1}\cdot M_{f}\cdot B. \]
