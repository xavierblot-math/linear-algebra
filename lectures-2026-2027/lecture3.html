Lecture 3 : sum, direct sum and complements

In the last lectures we introduced the notion of vector space and vector subspace, span, together with basic facts. In this lecture we study fundamental operations we can perform with vector spaces.

Intersection of vector subspaces

Intersection of vector subspaces

Let \(F,G\subset E\) be vector subspaces of a vector space \( E\). Then \(F\cap G\) is also a vector subspace of \( E\). Indeed:

  • \(0\in F\cap G\).
  • If \(x,y\in F\cap G\), then \(x+y\in F\) and \(x+y\in G\), since both \( F\) and \( G\) are vector subspaces.
  • For any \(\lambda\in\mathbb{K}\) and \( x\in F\cap G\), \(\lambda x\in F\) and \(\lambda x\in G\).

More generally, for an arbitrary family \(\{F_i\}_{i\in I}\) of subspaces of \( E\), by the same token, the intersection \(\displaystyle \bigcap_{i\in I} F_i\) is a subspace of \( E\).

Example: coordinate planes in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)

In \(E=\mathbb{R}^3\), the \(xy\)-plane is the subspace \(F=\mathbb{R}^2\times\{0\}\), and the \(yz\)-plane is the subspace \(G=\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\). They intersect in the $y$-axis, namely \(F\cap G=\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}\times\{0\}\).

Sum of vector spaces

Definition

For subspaces \(F,G\subset E\), we define \[ F+G \;:=\; \{\,u+v\in E \mid u\in F,\ v\in G\,\}, \] and we call it the sum of \( F\) and \(G\).

Lemma
Let the notation be as above.
  1. \(F+G\) is a subspace of \(E\).
  2. \(F\subset F+G\) and \(G\subset F+G\).
  3. Universal property: if \(H\subset E\) is a subspace with \(F\subset H\) and \(G\subset H\), then \(F+G\subset H\).

Proof

Below we use letters \(u\) for vectors in \(F\) and letters \(v\) for vectors in \(G\).

(a) We have \(0=0+0\in F+G\) and \((u_1+v_1)+(u_2+v_2)=(u_1+u_2)+(v_1+v_2)\in F+G\). Similarly, for every \(\lambda\in\mathbb{K}\) we have \(\lambda(u+v)=(\lambda u)+(\lambda v)\in F+G\).

(b) Clear since \(u=u+0\) and \(v=0+v\).

(c) If \(F,G\subset H\) then \(u+v\in H\), hence \(F+G\subset H\).

Generalization: sum of a family of vector subspaces

For subspaces \(\{F_i\}_{i\in I}\) of $E$, we define \[ \sum_{i\in I}F_i \;:=\; \left\{\,u_{i_1}+\cdots+u_{i_\ell}\ \middle|\ u_{i_j}\in F_{i_j}\ \text{for some finite } \{i_1,\dots,i_\ell\}\subset I \right\}, \]

and we call it the sum of the subspaces \(\{F_i\}_{i\in I}\). It satisfies analogous properties to the case of two subspaces.

Remark: \(F+G\) is not \(F\cup G\), but \(\mathrm{Span}(F\cup G)\)

Be careful not to confuse the sum \(F+G\) with the union \[ F\cup G=\{\,u\in E \mid u\in F \text{ or } u\in G\,\}. \] In general, \(F\cup G\) is not a vector subspace of \(E\). For instance, in \(E=\mathbb{R}^2\), let \(F=\mathbb{R}\times\{0\}\) be the \(x\)-axis and \(G=\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}\) the \(y\)-axis. Then \((1,0)\in F\) and \((0,1)\in G\), but \[ (1,0)+(0,1)=(1,1)\notin F\cup G . \] On the other hand, every \((x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2\) can be written \((x,y)=(x,0)+(0,y)\), so \(F+G=\mathbb{R}^2\).

The correct statement is \[ F+G=\mathrm{Span}(F\cup G). \] Indeed:

  • \(F+G\) is a subspace containing \(F\) and \(G\), hence containing \(F\cup G\). Since \(\mathrm{Span}(F\cup G)\) is the smallest subspace containing \(F\cup G\), we get \(\mathrm{Span}(F\cup G)\subset F+G\).
  • Conversely, any \(u+v\) with \(u\in F\), \(v\in G\) is a linear combination of elements of \(F\cup G\), so \(F+G\subset \mathrm{Span}(F\cup G)\).

In other words, \(F+G\) is the smallest subspace of \(E\) containing both \(F\) and \(G\) (this is exactly the universal property (c) above). By the same token, for a family of subspaces, \(\displaystyle \sum_{i\in I}F_i=\mathrm{Span}\Big(\bigcup_{i\in I}F_i\Big)\).

Example: the three coordinate axes in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)

In \(\mathbb{R}^3\), let \(F=\mathbb{R}\times\{0\}\times\{0\}\) be the \(x\)-axis, \(G=\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}\times\{0\}\) the \(y\)-axis and \(H=\{0\}\times\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}\) the \(z\)-axis. Then:

  • \(F+G=\{(x,y,0)\mid x,y\in\mathbb{R}\}\) is the \(xy\)-plane, since \((x,y,0)=(x,0,0)+(0,y,0)\);
  • \(F+H\) is the \(xz\)-plane;
  • \(G+H\) is the \(yz\)-plane;
  • \(F+G+H=\mathbb{R}^3\), since \((x,y,z)=(x,0,0)+(0,y,0)+(0,0,z)\).
Example: sum of coordinate planes and axes in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)

In \(\mathbb{R}^3\), the sum of any two distinct coordinate planes is \(\mathbb{R}^3\). For instance, we can write \[ (x,y,z)=(x,y,0)+(0,0,z), \] showing that \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\) is the sum of the \(xy\)-plane and the \(yz\)-plane. By the same token, \(\mathbb{R}^3\) is the sum of the \(xy\)-plane and the \(z\)-axis.

Example: two more interesting planes whose sum is \(\mathbb{R}^3\)

Let \[ F=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^3\mid x+2y=0\},\qquad G=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^3\mid x-y+z=0\}. \] We claim that \(F+G=\mathbb{R}^3\).

Step 1: description of \(F\). A vector \((x,y,z)\) lies in \(F\) if and only if \(x=-2y\). Hence any vector of \(F\) is of the form \[ \begin{pmatrix} -2y\\ y\\ z\end{pmatrix} = y\begin{pmatrix} -2\\ 1\\ 0\end{pmatrix} + z\begin{pmatrix} 0\\ 0\\ 1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad y,z\in\mathbb{R}, \] that is, \[ F=\mathrm{Span}\left(\begin{pmatrix} -2\\ 1\\ 0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix} 0\\ 0\\ 1\end{pmatrix}\right). \]

Step 2: description of \(G\). A vector \((x,y,z)\) lies in \(G\) if and only if \(y=x+z\). Hence any vector of \(G\) is of the form \[ \begin{pmatrix} x\\ x+z\\ z\end{pmatrix} = x\begin{pmatrix} 1\\ 1\\ 0\end{pmatrix} + z\begin{pmatrix} 0\\ 1\\ 1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad x,z\in\mathbb{R}, \] that is, \[ G=\mathrm{Span}\left(\begin{pmatrix} 1\\ 1\\ 0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix} 0\\ 1\\ 1\end{pmatrix}\right). \]

Step 3: reduction to the standard basis vectors. Any vector of \(\mathbb{R}^3\) is a linear combination of \[ e_1=\begin{pmatrix} 1\\ 0\\ 0\end{pmatrix},\quad e_2=\begin{pmatrix} 0\\ 1\\ 0\end{pmatrix},\quad e_3=\begin{pmatrix} 0\\ 0\\ 1\end{pmatrix}, \] i.e. \(\mathbb{R}^3=\mathrm{Span}(e_1,e_2,e_3)\). Since \(F+G\) is a vector subspace, it is stable under linear combinations. Therefore, to show \(F+G=\mathbb{R}^3\), it is enough to show that \(e_1,e_2,e_3\in F+G\).

Step 4: \(e_1,e_2,e_3\in F+G\).

  • \(e_3\): we have \(e_3\in F\subset F+G\) directly from Step 1.
  • \(e_1\): we have \[ e_1=\frac13\begin{pmatrix} 1\\ 1\\ 0\end{pmatrix}-\frac13\begin{pmatrix} -2\\ 1\\ 0\end{pmatrix}, \] where the first vector lies in \(G\) and the second in \(F\). Hence \(e_1\in F+G\).
  • \(e_2\): we have \[ e_2=\frac23\begin{pmatrix} 1\\ 1\\ 0\end{pmatrix}+\frac13\begin{pmatrix} -2\\ 1\\ 0\end{pmatrix}, \] where again the first vector lies in \(G\) and the second in \(F\). Hence \(e_2\in F+G\).

We conclude that \(\mathbb{R}^3=\mathrm{Span}(e_1,e_2,e_3)\subseteq F+G\), and therefore \(F+G=\mathbb{R}^3\).

Direct sums

Above we saw that $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ is the sum of the $xy$-plane and the $yz$-plane, but also the sum of the $xy$-plane and the $z$-axis. There is a substantial difference between these two situations. The $xy$-plane and the $yz$-plane intersect along the $y$-axis, i.e. they share a direction. By contrast, the intersection of the $xy$-plane and the $z$-axis is reduced to \(\{0\}\). These spaces do not share any direction. In such a situation, we speak of direct sum.

Lemma
Let the notation be as above. The following conditions are equivalent:
  1. \(F\cap G=\{0\}\).
  2. For all $u\in F+G$, there exist unique $f\in F$ and $g\in G$ such that $u=f+g$.

Proof

(a)\(\Rightarrow\)(b). Suppose first that the intersection is $0$. Let $u=f+g$, with $f\in F$ and $g\in G$. Suppose we have another such decomposition $u=f^{\prime}+g^{\prime}$. Then \[ f-f^{\prime}=g^{\prime}-g. \] Now, the left hand side belongs to $F$, and the right hand side belongs to $G$. Hence, both sides belong to $F\cap G$. Since the latter is zero, we infer $f=f^{\prime}$ and $g=g^{\prime}$.

(b)\(\Rightarrow\)(a). If $u\in F\cap G$, then we decompose $u=u+0=0+u$. Using the uniqueness of the decomposition, we conclude that $u=0$.

Definition

Let $F, G$ be subspaces of a vector space $E$. If any of the equivalent conditions of the previous lemma holds, then we say that $F$ and $G$ form a direct sum. In this case, we write $$F\oplus G$$ instead of $F+G$. The symbol $\oplus$ hence means $+$ together with the condition $F\cap G=\lbrace 0\rbrace$.

Examples: planes and axes in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), revisited

  • The \(xy\)-plane and the \(yz\)-plane are not in direct sum. They share the \(y\)-axis, and indeed a vector of \(\mathbb{R}^3\) admits several decompositions, e.g. \[ (x,y,z)=\underbrace{(x,0,0)}_{xy\text{-plane}}+\underbrace{(0,y,z)}_{yz\text{-plane}} =\underbrace{(x,y,0)}_{xy\text{-plane}}+\underbrace{(0,0,z)}_{yz\text{-plane}}. \] These are two different decompositions as soon as \(y\neq 0\).

  • The \(xy\)-plane and the \(z\)-axis are in direct sum. Their intersection is \(\{0\}\), and the decomposition \[ (x,y,z)=(x,y,0)+(0,0,z) \] is unique. Hence \(\mathbb{R}^3=(xy\text{-plane})\oplus(z\text{-axis})\).

Intuition. \(\mathbb{R}^3\) has \(3\) independent directions, while each of the \(xy\)-plane and the \(yz\)-plane has \(2\). Since \(2+2=4>3\), the two planes are forced to share a direction. By contrast, \(2+1=3\) for the \(xy\)-plane and the \(z\)-axis. This will be made precise with the notion of dimension.

Examples

  1. Recall: a polynomial of degree \(d \geq 0\) in \(\mathbb{K}[X]\) has at most \(d\) distinct roots.

    In \(\mathbb{K}[X]_{\leq 3}\), let:

    • \(F = \{ P \in \mathbb{K}[X]_{\leq 3} \mid P(0) = P(1) = 0 \}\)
    • \(G = \{ P \in \mathbb{K}[X]_{\leq 3} \mid P(2) = P(3) = 0 \}\)

    Then, by the property above, \(F \cap G = \{0\}\): a polynomial in \(F\cap G\) has degree at most \(3\) and vanishes at the four points \(0,1,2,3\). (This uses that \(0,1,2,3\) are distinct in \(\mathbb{K}\), which holds for \(\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\). Over \(\mathbb{F}_2\), however, \(2=0\) and \(3=1\), so \(F=G\).)

    As a tedious exercise, you can try to show that \[ F \oplus G = \mathbb{K}[X]_{\leq 3}. \] We will later provide useful tools (dimension theory) which provide a simple approach.

  2. In \(C^0(\mathbb{R}) = \{ f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R} \ \text{continuous}\}\), let:

    • \(F_0 = \{ f \in C^0(\mathbb{R}) \mid f(0)=0 \}\)
    • \(F_1 = \{ f \in C^0(\mathbb{R}) \mid f(1)=0 \}\)

    Then \(F_0 \cap F_1 \neq \{0\}\), since \(f(x) = \sin(\pi x)\) belongs to both \(F_0\) and \(F_1\), and \(f \neq 0\).

    As an exercise, try to show that \[ F_0 + F_1 = C^0(\mathbb{R}). \]

    Hint: for \(f\in C^0(\mathbb{R})\), write \[ f=\big(f-f(0)\,(1-x)\big)+f(0)\,(1-x), \] and check which of \(F_0\), \(F_1\) each term belongs to.

Complements of vector subspaces

Definition

Let \(E\) be a vector space and \(F \subseteq E\) a vector subspace. A complement for \(F\) is a vector subspace \(G \subseteq E\) such that

\[ E = F \oplus G. \]

We then say that \(F\) and \(G\) are complementary vector subspaces.

Example

In \(\mathbb{R}^3\), let \(F\) be the \(xy\)-plane and \(G\) the \(z\)-axis. We saw above that \(\mathbb{R}^3=F\oplus G\), so \(F\) and \(G\) are complementary subspaces.

A complement is not unique: for instance, the line \(\mathrm{Span}\big((1,1,1)\big)\) is also a complement of the \(xy\)-plane, since \((x,y,z)=(x-z,\,y-z,\,0)+z\,(1,1,1)\) and this line meets the \(xy\)-plane only at \(0\). More generally, any line not contained in the \(xy\)-plane is a complement of it.

Proposition

Let \(E\) be a vector space. Then any vector subspace \(F \subset E\) admits a complement.

Proof

In general, a proof based on Zorn's Lemma is proposed in Exercise 13 of Exercise Sheet 3.

We will give another proof later when we discuss dimensions and bases, for finite-dimensional vector spaces.

Going further

Direct sums of more than two subspaces

This material was not discussed in the lectures and is not required. It is included for curious readers. For a related exercise, see Ex. 7 in Sheet 3.

Let \(F_1,\dots,F_n\) be subspaces of \(E\). We say that they form a direct sum, and write \[ F_1\oplus\cdots\oplus F_n \] instead of \(F_1+\cdots+F_n\), if every \(u\in F_1+\cdots+F_n\) can be written uniquely as \(u=u_1+\cdots+u_n\) with \(u_i\in F_i\). As in the case of two subspaces, one shows (exercise) that this is equivalent to either of the following conditions:

  • if \(u_1+\cdots+u_n=0\) with \(u_i\in F_i\), then \(u_1=\cdots=u_n=0\);
  • for every \(k\), \(\displaystyle F_k\cap\Big(\sum_{j\neq k}F_j\Big)=\{0\}\).

Warning. It is not enough to require that the subspaces intersect pairwise trivially, i.e. \(F_i\cap F_j=\{0\}\) for \(i\neq j\). For instance, in \(\mathbb{R}^2\), consider the three lines \[ F_1=\mathrm{Span}\big((1,0)\big),\quad F_2=\mathrm{Span}\big((0,1)\big),\quad F_3=\mathrm{Span}\big((1,1)\big). \] Any two of them intersect in \(\{0\}\), but \[ (1,1)=\underbrace{(1,0)}_{F_1}+\underbrace{(0,1)}_{F_2}+\underbrace{0}_{F_3}=\underbrace{0}_{F_1}+\underbrace{0}_{F_2}+\underbrace{(1,1)}_{F_3} \] gives two different decompositions. Hence \(F_1,F_2,F_3\) are not in direct sum. Equivalently, \(F_3\cap(F_1+F_2)=F_3\neq\{0\}\).
