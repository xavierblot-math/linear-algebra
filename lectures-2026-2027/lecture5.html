Lecture 5: Bases and dimension

In the previous lectures, we studied linear independence, the span of a family of vectors, and the notion of dimension. These notions are in fact closely related: for example, we saw that in a vector space of dimension $n$, any spanning family of cardinality $n$ is linearly independent. We now study such special families of vectors and, along the way, develop tools to determine the dimension of a vector space.

Examples and definition

First examples of bases

In several examples, such as $\mathbb K^n$ and $\mathbb K[X]$, we have exhibited families of vectors with the following two fundamental properties:

  1. Spanning family.
    $$\mathbb K^n=\langle (1,0,\ldots,0),\ldots,(0,\ldots,0,1)\rangle,\qquad \mathbb K[X]=\langle 1,X,X^2,\ldots\rangle.$$
    Hence any vector can be written as a (finite) linear combination of these vectors.
  2. Linear independence. These spanning families are also linearly independent. Consequently, any vector can be written uniquely as a linear combination of these vectors, up to order.

These are examples of bases. Bases can be seen as the building blocks of a vector space: they encapsulate all of its algebraic structure.

Definition (Basis)

Let $E$ be a $\mathbb K$-vector space. A basis of $E$ is a family of vectors $(e_i)_{i\in I}$ of $E$ such that:

  1. it is a spanning family of $E$, that is, $E=\langle (e_i)_{i\in I}\rangle$;
  2. it is linearly independent.

Equivalently, every $v\in E$ can be written in a unique way as a linear combination of the $e_i$. More precisely,

$$v=\lambda_1 e_{i_1}+\cdots+\lambda_n e_{i_n},$$

with $i_1,\ldots,i_n\in I$ distinct and $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n\in\mathbb K$, and this decomposition is unique up to order and up to terms with coefficient $0$.

The scalars $\lambda_k$ are called the coordinates of $v$ in the basis $(e_i)_{i\in I}$.
