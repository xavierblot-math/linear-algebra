Lecture 1 : groups, vector spaces and vector subspaces

What is Linear Algebra about?

Linear algebra provides general concepts and tools for tackling systems of linear equations.

Definition (linear equation)

A linear equation in the variables $x_1,\dots,x_n$ is an equation of the form

$$a_1x_1+a_2x_2+\cdots+a_nx_n=b$$

where the coefficients $a_1,\dots,a_n,b$ are numbers in $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$ .

Examples
  • $3x_1+5x_2=7$  is a linear equation.
  • $x_1x_2=3$  is not a linear equation.
  • $\cos(x)=7$  is not a linear equation either.
Definition (system of linear equations)
A system of linear equations is a collection of linear equations.
Example
\[ \begin{cases} x + y - 2z = 1,\\ x - 2y + z = 1,\\ 2x - y - z = 2, \end{cases} \qquad (x,y,z)\ \text{unknowns.} \]

See GeoGebra 3D or Desmos 3D if you want to visualize.

Some key questions
  • Do solutions exist?
  • If so, are they unique?
  • What is the structure of the solution space?
  • What is the "size" of the solution space?

We will reformulate these questions using the language of matrices and vectors:

\[ \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&-2\\1&-2&1\\2&-1&-1\end{pmatrix}}_{\text{matrix}}\hspace{0.7cm}\cdot \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix}}_{\text{unknown vector}} = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\2\end{pmatrix}}_{\text{given vector}}. \]

Matrices, vectors, and linear maps

The above questions depend on the properties of the matrix and the given vector. We will develop a general theory to study matrices and vectors.

The previous equation appears as a particular case of the equation \[f(u)=v.\] Here \(u,v\) are vectors in a vector space. In our example \(u=\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix}\), \(v=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\2\end{pmatrix}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

The function \(f\) is a linear map between vector spaces: \[ f\!\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1&-1&-2\\1&-2&1\\2&-1&-1\end{pmatrix}\cdot \begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix}. \]

From general theory to concrete problems

Why is Linear Algebra relevant?

Many practical problems are of this form, or can be studied by reducing them to this form. It is thus important to identify general facts which are common and apply to all these situations.

Examples of contexts
  • Mathematics itself: linear problems are the ones we control completely, so the standard move elsewhere is to attach a vector space to your problem and work there instead.
  • Quantum mechanics: states are vectors, observables are matrices, and the energy levels of an atom are eigenvalues.
  • Neural networks: each layer is a matrix applied to a vector.
  • Economics, Leontief's input–output model: dependence of several sectors on each other.
  • Chemistery: finding the ratio of molecules in a chemical reaction.
  • Weather forecasting: the equations of the atmosphere are not linear, this is why the weather is unpredictable. But a forecast is computed by replacing them, at every time step, by an enormous linear system.
  • ...

Groups

Definition

A group is a non-empty set \(G\) together with a binary operation \( *:G\times G\to G,\ (g,h)\mapsto g*h\), satisfying:

  • Identity element: there exists \(e\in G\) such that \(g*e=e*g=g\) for all \(g\in G\).
  • Associativity: \((g_1*g_2)*g_3=g_1*(g_2*g_3)\) for all \(g_1,g_2,g_3\in G\).
  • Inverse element: for each \(g\in G\) there exists \(h\in G\) with \(g*h=h*g=e\).

The group is commutative (or abelian) if \(g*h=h*g\) for all \(g,h\in G\).

Notation
  • The operation \( * \) is often written \(+\) or \(\cdot\).
  • The identity \(e\) is written \(0\) (for \(+\)) or \(1\) (for \(\cdot\)).
  • For \(+\) we say opposite rather than inverse.
  • Thanks to associativity, parentheses can be omitted.
Examples/non-examples
  1. \(\{0\}\) with \(+\).
  2. \(\mathbb{Z}\) with \(+\) and identity \(0\) (abelian).
  3. \(\mathbb{N}\) with \(+\): not a group (no additive inverses).
  4. \(\mathbb{R}^\times=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\) with \(\cdot\) and identity \(1\). If \(x\neq 0\), then \(x\cdot\frac{1}{x}=\frac{1}{x}\cdot x=1\).
  5. \(\mathbb{R}\) with \(\cdot\) is not a group, since \(0\) is not invertible.
Clock arithmetic (mod 12)

Add as usual, move the hand clockwise, and read the new hour.

\[ 3+11=14\equiv 2 \pmod{12}, \quad 3+9=12\equiv 0 \pmod{12}. \]

Thus the opposite of \(3\) is \(9\).

Lemma

Let \(G\) be a group. Then:

  1. The identity element is unique.
  2. Inverses are unique.

Proof. (a) If \(e_1,e_2\) are both identities, then \(e_1*e_2=e_2\) and \(e_1*e_2=e_1\), hence \(e_1=e_2\).
(b) If \(h_1,h_2\) are inverses of \(g\), write \((h_1*g)*h_2 = h_1*(g*h_2)\). Using \(g*h_2=e\) and \(h_1*g=e\), we get \(h_1=h_2\).

Notation
  • The inverse of \(g\) is written \(g^{-1}\) and satisfies \(g*g^{-1}=g^{-1}*g=e\).
  • In additive notation (commutative groups), the inverse of \(g\) is written \(-g\).

Vector spaces

Let us begin with an example. Recall \(\mathbb{R}^2 = \{(x,y)\mid x,y\in \mathbb{R}\}\).

  • Addition: \((x,y)+(x',y')=(x+x',y+y')\).
  • Zero element: \((0,0)+(x,y)=(x,y)\).
  • Inverse: \(-(x,y)=(-x,-y)\).

We see that \(\mathbb{R}^2\) with \(+\) and \(0=(0,0)\) is a commutative group.

But we can do something more: for \(\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\), define a product by \(\lambda\cdot(x,y)=(\lambda x,\lambda y)\). This satisfies distributivity over the sum and other nice properties, giving an “interaction” between product and addition.

Notation

From now on, \(\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{R},\) or \(\mathbb{C}\). More generally, \(\mathbb{K}\) can be taken to be any field, although we won't discuss fields in these lectures.

Definition

A \(\mathbb{K}\)-vector space (or vector space over \(\mathbb{K}\)) is a non-empty set \(E\) together with:

  1. A commutative group structure \((E,+)\) with neutral element \(0_E\).
  2. Multiplication by scalars: \(\mathbb{K}\times E \to E,\ (\lambda,u)\mapsto \lambda \cdot u\), satisfying:
    • Distributivity over vectors: \(\lambda\cdot (u+v)=\lambda \cdot u+\lambda \cdot v\).
    • Distributivity over scalars: \((\lambda+\mu)\cdot u=\lambda \cdot u+\mu \cdot u\).
    • Associativity: \((\lambda\cdot\mu)\cdot u=\lambda\cdot (\mu \cdot u)\).
    • Identity: \(1\cdot u=u\).

The elements of \(E\) are usually called vectors. The elements of $\mathbb{K}$ are called scalars.

Examples
  1. \(\{0\}\) with \(0+0=0\) and \(\lambda\cdot 0=0\) (“zero vector space”).
  2. \(\mathbb{K}^n=\{(a_1,\dots,a_n)\mid a_i\in\mathbb{K}\}\), with componentwise addition and scalar multiplication. In particular, \(\mathbb{K}\) itself is a vector space over \(\mathbb{K}\).
  3. Sequences: \(\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{N}}=\{(a_n)_{n\ge0}\mid a_n\in\mathbb{K}\}\), with componentwise addition and scalar multiplication.
  4. Polynomials: the space $\mathbb{K}[X]$ of polynomials with coefficient in $\mathbb{K}$ (see Exercice sheet 1).
  5. The space of $2\times2$ matrices with coefficient in $\mathbb{K}$ (see Exercice sheet 1)
  6. If \(X\) is a set, the space of functions \(\mathcal{F}(X,\mathbb{K})=\{f:X\to \mathbb{K}\}\) with addition \((f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x)\), zero \(0(x)=0\), inverse \((-f)(x)=-f(x)\), and scalar multiplication \((\lambda f)(x)=\lambda f(x)\). By convention, if \( X\) is empty, we define \(\mathcal{F}(X,\mathbb{K})\) to be the zero vector space.
