Lecture 5 : bases and dimension

In the previous lectures we have been dealing with linear independence, the span of a family of vectors, and the notion of dimension. These notions are actually related. For example, we saw that in a vector space of dimension $n$, any spanning family of cardinality $n$ is linearly independent. We wish to study such special families of vectors, and along the way provide tools to determine the dimension of a vector space.

Examples and definition

First examples of bases

In many examples such as $\mathbb K^n$ and $\mathbb K[X]$, we have exhibited families of vectors with the aforementioned fundamental properties:

  1. Spanning family.
    $$\mathbb K^n=\langle (1,0,\ldots,0),\ldots,(0,\ldots,0,1)\rangle,\qquad \mathbb K[X]=\langle 1,X,X^2,\ldots\rangle.$$
    Hence any vector can be written as a combination of such vectors.
  2. Linear independence. The displayed spanning families are also linearly independent. Consequently, any vector can be uniquely written in terms of such vectors, up to order.

These are examples of bases, and they can be seen as the building blocks of a vector space, which encapsulate all of its algebraic structure.

Definition (Basis)

Let $E$ be a $\mathbb K$-vector space. A basis of $E$ is a family of vectors $(e_i)_{i\in I}$ such that:

  1. It is a spanning family for $E$, that is $E=\langle (e_i)_{i\in I}\rangle$;
  2. It is linearly independent.

Equivalently, every $v\in E$ can be written in a unique way as a linear combination of the $e_i$. Precisely, $$v=\lambda_1 e_{i_1}+\cdots+\lambda_n e_{i_n}$$ with $i_1,\ldots,i_n\in I$ distinct and $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n\in\mathbb K$, and this decomposition is unique up to order and terms with coefficient $0$.

The $\lambda_i$ are called the coordinates of $v$ in the basis.

Remark

The two conditions of the definition correspond exactly to the two halves of "there exists a unique decomposition":

  • Existence ⟺ (a): every $v\in E$ is of the form $\lambda_1 e_{i_1}+\cdots+\lambda_n e_{i_n}$.
  • Uniqueness ⟺ (b): if $\lambda_1 e_{i_1}+\cdots+\lambda_n e_{i_n}=\mu_1 e_{i_1}+\cdots+\mu_n e_{i_n}$ with $i_1,\ldots,i_n$ distinct, then $\lambda_k=\mu_k$ for all $k$. Indeed, this means $\sum_k(\lambda_k-\mu_k)e_{i_k}=0 \Rightarrow \lambda_k-\mu_k=0$.

Two decompositions of $v$ may involve different indices, but adding terms with coefficient $0$ we can always write them on the same indices $i_1,\ldots,i_n$.

Examples
  1. In $\mathbb K^n$, the vectors $$e_1=(1,0,\ldots,0),\quad e_2=(0,1,0,\ldots,0),\quad\ldots,\quad e_n=(0,\ldots,0,1)$$ form a basis, called the canonical basis (or standard basis) of $\mathbb K^n$. The coordinates of $v=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$ in this basis are its components: $v=x_1e_1+\cdots+x_ne_n$.
  2. In $\mathbb K[X]$, the monomials $1,X,X^2,\ldots$, that is the family $(X^k)_{k\in\mathbb N}$, form a basis. The coordinates of a polynomial $P=a_0+a_1X+\cdots+a_dX^d$ are its coefficients $a_0,\ldots,a_d$.
Example (One vector, two bases)

In $\mathbb R^2$, consider the vector $v=(3,2)$ and two bases: the canonical basis $e_1=(1,0)$, $e_2=(0,1)$, and the basis $f_1=(1,0)$, $f_2=(1,1)$.

The vector $v$ is the same in both pictures; only the grid used to measure it changes.

  • In the canonical basis, $v=3e_1+2e_2$: its coordinates $(3,2)$ are simply its components.
  • In the basis $(f_1,f_2)$, $v=f_1+2f_2$: its coordinates are $(1,2)$.

A basis is a choice of grid: every point of the plane is reached in exactly one way by stepping along the basis vectors, and the numbers of steps are the coordinates.

Existence, cardinality and dimension

Theorem (Existence of bases)

Every vector space admits a basis.

Proof

If $E$ is finite dimensional with $\dim E=n$ and $E=\langle e_1,\ldots,e_n\rangle$, we know from the previous lecture that $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ are linearly independent, hence form a basis. For general $E$, not necessarily finite dimensional, we admit the result. See Exercise Sheet 3.

Theorem (Dimension and cardinality of bases)

Let $E$ be a finite dimensional $\mathbb K$-vector space. Suppose $\dim E=n$. Then any basis has exactly $n$ elements.

The proof of this theorem rests on a single statement, the exchange lemma, which compares linearly independent families with spanning families.

The exchange lemma

Lemma (Steinitz exchange lemma)

Let $E$ be a vector space with $E=\langle e_1,\ldots,e_n\rangle$, and let $u_1,\ldots,u_m\in E$ be linearly independent. Then $m\leq n$, and after renumbering the vectors $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ we have $$E=\langle u_1,\ldots,u_m,\,e_{m+1},\ldots,e_n\rangle.$$

In words: the vectors $u_i$ can be brought into the spanning family one at a time, each of them taking the place of some $e_j$, without ever losing the spanning property.

Remark (Renumbering)

"Renumbering" means that the vectors $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ are listed in a different order. The order of a family does not change its span, but it does change which vectors are called $e_{m+1},\ldots,e_n$, that is, which vectors are kept. The right order depends on the $u_i$. Without renumbering, the statement is false.

Example. In $\mathbb R^2=\langle e_1,e_2\rangle$ with $e_1=(1,0)$, $e_2=(0,1)$, take $u_1=(0,1)$. Then $$\langle u_1,e_2\rangle=\langle e_2\rangle\neq\mathbb R^2,$$ because $u_1=e_2$. Renumber: call $e'_1=e_2$ the first vector and $e'_2=e_1$ the second. Then $$\mathbb R^2=\langle e'_1,e'_2\rangle=\langle u_1,e'_2\rangle.$$ Here the renumbering is forced: $u_1=0\cdot e_1+1\cdot e_2$, and since the coefficient of $e_1$ is zero, $u_1$ can only take the place of $e_2$.

Formally, renumbering means: there is a permutation $\sigma$ of $\{1,\ldots,n\}$ such that $E=\langle u_1,\ldots,u_m,\,e_{\sigma(m+1)},\ldots,e_{\sigma(n)}\rangle$.

Proof of the exchange lemma

We proceed by induction. Fix $u_1,\ldots,u_m$. For $k=0,1,\ldots,m$, consider the statement $$\boxed{\;P(k):\quad k\le n\ \text{ and, after renumbering the } e_j,\quad E=\langle u_1,\ldots,u_k,\,e_{k+1},\ldots,e_n\rangle.\;}$$ The lemma is $P(m)$. Concretely:

  • $P(0)$: $0\le n$ and $E=\langle e_1,\ldots,e_n\rangle$;
  • $P(1)$: $1\le n$ and $E=\langle u_1,e_2,\ldots,e_n\rangle$;
  • $P(2)$: $2\le n$ and $E=\langle u_1,u_2,e_3,\ldots,e_n\rangle$;
  • $\ \vdots$

Each step brings in one more vector $u_k$, which takes the place of one of the remaining $e_j$.

Base case ($k=0$). $P(0)$ says $0\le n$ and $E=\langle e_1,\ldots,e_n\rangle$. This is the hypothesis: there is nothing to prove.

Induction step. Let $1\le k\le m$ and assume $P(k-1)$, that is $k-1\le n$ and, after renumbering, $$E=\langle u_1,\ldots,u_{k-1},\,e_k,\ldots,e_n\rangle.$$ We prove $P(k)$.

  1. Decompose. Since $u_k\in E$, by $P(k-1)$ there are scalars $a_i,b_j$ such that $$u_k=a_1u_1+\cdots+a_{k-1}u_{k-1}+b_ke_k+\cdots+b_ne_n.\tag{$*$}$$
  2. Some $b_j\neq0$. Otherwise $u_k\in\langle u_1,\ldots,u_{k-1}\rangle$, which contradicts the linear independence of $u_1,\ldots,u_m$.
  3. Exchange. Renumber $e_k,\ldots,e_n$ so that $b_k\neq0$. The vectors $u_1,\ldots,u_{k-1}$, already in place, are not touched. Then $(*)$ gives $$e_k=\frac{1}{b_k}\Big(u_k-a_1u_1-\cdots-a_{k-1}u_{k-1}-b_{k+1}e_{k+1}-\cdots-b_ne_n\Big).$$ Let $F=\langle u_1,\ldots,u_k,\,e_{k+1},\ldots,e_n\rangle$.
    • $F\subseteq E$, since all these vectors belong to $E$.
    • $e_k\in F$ by the formula above. So $F$ contains $u_1,\ldots,u_{k-1},e_k,\ldots,e_n$, which span $E$ by $P(k-1)$. Since a subspace containing some vectors contains their span, $E\subseteq F$.
    Hence $E=F=\langle u_1,\ldots,u_k,\,e_{k+1},\ldots,e_n\rangle$.
  4. Check the inequality $k\le n$. By $P(k-1)$, $k-1\le n$. Suppose $k-1=n$, that is $k>n$. Then $P(k-1)$ reads $E=\langle u_1,\ldots,u_{k-1}\rangle$, so $u_k\in\langle u_1,\ldots,u_{k-1}\rangle$, which contradicts linear independence. Hence $k\le n$.

This proves $P(k)$.

Conclusion. By induction, $P(k)$ holds for all $0\le k\le m$. In particular $P(m)$, which is the lemma, holds. $\square$

Example

In $E=\mathbb{R}^3=\langle e_1,e_2,e_3\rangle$ (canonical basis), take the linearly independent vectors $u_1=(1,1,0)$ and $u_2=(0,1,1)$.

  1. $u_1=e_1+e_2$. The coefficient of $e_1$ is non-zero, so $e_1=u_1-e_2$ and $\mathbb{R}^3=\langle u_1,e_2,e_3\rangle$.
  2. $u_2=0\cdot u_1+e_2+e_3$. The coefficient of $e_2$ is non-zero, so $e_2=u_2-e_3$ and $\mathbb{R}^3=\langle u_1,u_2,e_3\rangle$.
Remark

The vectors $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ are only assumed to span $E$: they need not be linearly independent. The lemma says that a linearly independent family can never be larger than a spanning family.

Consequences

We now draw the consequences of the exchange lemma. They come in three steps: the cardinality of bases (which proves the Theorem), criteria to recognize a basis by counting, and the dimension of subspaces.

1. Cardinality of bases

Proof of the Theorem (Dimension and cardinality of bases)

By the theorem on existence of bases (see its proof), $E$ has a basis $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ with $n=\dim E$. Let $(u_i)_{i\in I}$ be another basis of $E$.

$I$ is finite and $|I|\leq n$. Every finite subfamily of $(u_i)_{i\in I}$ is linearly independent and lies in $E=\langle e_1,\ldots,e_n\rangle$, hence has at most $n$ elements by the exchange lemma. Thus $I$ is finite; write $u_1,\ldots,u_m$ for this basis, with $m\leq n$.

$n\leq m$. Exchange the roles of the two families: $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ are linearly independent and $E=\langle u_1,\ldots,u_m\rangle$, so the exchange lemma gives $n\leq m$.

Hence $m=n$. $\square$

This gives a simple way to compute the dimension of a vector space: construct a basis, and count its elements.

Corollary (Dimension of a span)

Let $E$ be a vector space and $e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}\in E$ linearly independent vectors. Then $e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}$ is a basis of $\langle e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}\rangle$ and $\dim \langle e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}\rangle=n$.

Proof

The first claim holds by definition of a basis. The space $\langle e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}\rangle$ is finite dimensional, so by the theorem its dimension is the cardinality of any of its bases, namely $n$. $\square$

2. Bases in a space of dimension $n$

Corollary (Counting criteria)

Let $E$ be a $\mathbb K$-vector space with $\dim E=n$.

  1. Every linearly independent family of $E$ has at most $n$ elements.
  2. Every spanning family of $E$ has at least $n$ elements.
  3. For a family $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ of exactly $n$ vectors of $E$, the following are equivalent:
    • $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ are linearly independent;
    • $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ span $E$;
    • $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ is a basis of $E$.
Proof

Let $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ be a basis of $E$.

(a) Apply the exchange lemma to a (finite subfamily of the) linearly independent family and to the spanning family $e_1,\ldots,e_n$.

(b) An infinite family has more than $n$ elements. If $E=\langle v_1,\ldots,v_p\rangle$, apply the exchange lemma to the linearly independent family $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ and to the spanning family $v_1,\ldots,v_p$: we get $n\leq p$.

(c) A spanning family of $n$ vectors is linearly independent by the previous lecture. Conversely, if $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ are linearly independent, the exchange lemma with $m=n$ exchanges all the $e_j$, so $E=\langle u_1,\ldots,u_n\rangle$. In both cases we get a basis, and a basis satisfies both conditions. $\square$

Remark

Once the dimension is known, half of the work is saved: to check that $n$ vectors form a basis of a space of dimension $n$, it is enough to check either linear independence or the spanning property.
