Lecture 9 : the dual vector space

The dual space and dual bases

Definition (dual vector space)

Let $E$ be a vector space over $\mathbb{K}$.

  1. A linear form on $E$ is a linear map $\omega\colon E\to\mathbb{K}$.
  2. The dual vector space of $E$, denoted by $E^{\ast}$ or also $E^{\vee}$, is the vector space of linear forms on $E$. Namely, $E^{\ast}=\mathcal{L}(E,\mathbb{K})$.
Examples
  1. Let $u=(u_{1},\ldots, u_{n})\in\mathbb{K}^{n}$. Then we can associate to it a linear form
    \begin{equation*} \begin{split} \mathbb{K}^{n}&\longrightarrow\mathbb{K}\\ \left(\begin{array}{c}x_{1}\\ \vdots\\ x_{n}\end{array}\right)&\longmapsto u_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+u_{n}x_{n}. \end{split} \end{equation*}
    Note that this is the linear map associated to $u$ seen as a matrix in $M_{1n}$.
  2. For a scalar $a\in\mathbb{K}$, we define a linear form on the space of polynomials by
    \begin{equation*} \begin{split} \mathrm{ev}_{a}\colon \mathbb{K}[X]&\longrightarrow\mathbb{K}\\ P(X)&\longmapsto P(a). \end{split} \end{equation*}
  3. Let $E$ a vector space, and suppose that $e\in E$ a non-zero vector. Let $F$ be a complement for the span $\langle e\rangle$, so that $E=\langle e\rangle\oplus F$. The projecting onto $\langle e\rangle$ provides a linear map
    \[ p\colon E\to\langle e\rangle. \]
    Choosing $e$ as a basis of $\langle e\rangle$ provides a bijective linear map
    \[ \iota\colon \langle e\rangle\to\mathbb{K}. \]
    Composing $\iota\circ p$, we obtain a linear form $\omega\colon E\to\mathbb{K}$. This can explicitly be described as follows: if $u\in E$, decompose $u=\lambda e+ v$, where $\lambda\in\mathbb{K}$ and $v\in F$ are uniquely determined; then $\omega(u)=\lambda$.

We next describe a key construction to produce a basis of a dual vector space from a basis of a vector space, in the finite dimensional setting.

Construction (dual basis)

Let $E$ be a finite dimensional vector space. Let $e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}\in E$ be a basis of $E$. For every $e_{i}$, we know that there exists a unique linear map $E\to\mathbb{K}$ which sends $e_{i}$ to $1$ and $e_{j}$ to $0$, for $j\neq i$. We denote by $e_{i}^{\ast}$ this linear map. Concretely, it is defined by

\[ e_{i}^{\ast}(a_{1}e_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}e_{n})=a_{i}. \]

Hence, $e_{i}^{\ast}$ gives the $i$-th coordinate of a vector in the basis $e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}$.

If $\omega\colon E\to\mathbb{K}$ is a linear form, we have

\[ \omega=\lambda_{1} e_{1}^{\ast}+\ldots+\lambda_{n}e_{n}^{\ast}, \]

where the scalars $\lambda_{i}$ are defined as $\lambda_{i}=\omega(e_{i})$. This can be proven simply by evaluating both side on the basis vectors $e_{i}$, and using that a linear map is determined by its values on a basis. This shows that $e_{1}^{\ast},\ldots, e_{n}^{\ast}$ span $E^{\ast}$. Moreover, they are linearly independent. For if we write a linear relationship

\[ a_{1}e_{1}^{\ast}+\cdots+a_{n}e_{n}^{\ast}=0, \]

then evaluating on $e_{i}$ we obtain $a_{i}=0$.

We conclude that if $e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}$ is a basis of $E$, then $e_{1}^{\ast},\ldots, e_{n}^{\ast}$ is a basis of $E^{\ast}$. Incidentally, we have proven that $\dim E^{\ast}=\dim E$.

We record the result of the previous construction in a proposition.

Proposition

Let $E$ be a finite dimensional vector space.

  1. If $e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}$ is a basis of $E$, then $e_{1}^{\ast},\ldots, e_{n}^{\ast}$ is a basis of $E^{\ast}$. In particular, $\dim E^{\ast}=\dim E$.
  2. If $\omega\colon E\to\mathbb{K}$ is a linear form, then the coefficients of $\omega$ in the basis $e_{1}^{\ast},\ldots, e_{n}^{\ast}$ are $(\omega(e_{1}),\ldots, \omega(e_{n}))$.

The transpose of a linear map

Lemma

Let $f\colon E\to F$ be a linear map of vector spaces. Define

\begin{equation*} \begin{split} f^{\ast}\colon F^{\ast}&\longrightarrow E^{\ast}\\ \omega&\longmapsto f^{\ast}(\omega):=\omega\circ f, \end{split} \end{equation*}

that is $f^{\ast}(\omega)\colon E\to\mathbb{K}$ sends $u\in E$ to $\omega(f(u))$. Then $f^{\ast}$ is a linear map.

Proof

This follows from the linearity of $f$, and the definition of the vector space structure on $E^{\ast}$.

Definition

Let $f\colon E\to F$ be a linear map of vector spaces. The linear map $f^{\ast}\colon F^{\ast}\to E^{\ast}$ defined as above is called the transpose or dual map of $f$.

The following statement shows that the matrix of the transpose of a linear map is the transpose of the matrix.

Proposition

Let $f\colon E\to F$ be a linear map of finite dimensional vector spaces. Let be given bases $e_{1},\ldots, e_{n}$ of $E$ and $u_{1},\ldots, u_{m}$ of $F$. Let $A=(a_{ij})_{ij}$ be the matrix of $f$ in these bases. Then the matrix of $f^{\ast}$ in the dual bases is the transpose of $A$, namely $^{t}A=(a_{ji})_{ij}$, obtained from $A$ by exchanging the roles of the rows and the columns.

Proof

We have to evaluate $f^{\ast}$ on the basis vectors $u^{\ast}_{1},\ldots, u_{m}^{\ast}$, and express the result in terms of the basis $e_{1}^{\ast},\ldots, e_{n}^{\ast}$. Hence, let us decompose

\[ f^{\ast}(u_{j}^{\ast})=b_{1j}e_{1}^{\ast}+b_{2j}e_{2}^{\ast}+\cdots+b_{nj}e_{n}^{\ast}. \]

This means that $(b_{ij})$ is the matrix of $f^{\ast}$ in the dual bases. This is an equality of linear maps. To determine both sides, we evaluate on $e_{i}$. The right hand side gives $b_{ij}$. For the right hand side, since $f(e_{i})=a_{1i}u_{1}+\cdots+a_{mi}u_{m}$, we find

\[ f^{\ast}(u_{j}^{\ast})(e_{i})=u_{j}^{\ast}(f(e_{i}))=u_{j}^{\ast}(a_{1i}u_{1}+\cdots+a_{mi}u_{m})=a_{ji}. \]

Hence, the matrix of $f^{\ast}$ is $(b_{ij})_{ij}=(a_{ji})_{ij}$ as claimed.
